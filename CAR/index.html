ICCSD · FY24 Audit Watchlist

When the FY24 audit drops, here's what to check first

The Iowa City Community School District's FY24 audit is expected by July 2026. It will be the first independent look at the district's books since the financial crisis surfaced. The state-filed FY24 report (the CAR) has several numbers that do not fit the four-year trend or the year that followed. This page walks through what to compare against the audit when it lands — and what each comparison would mean.

The two-minute version

Every Iowa school district files an annual financial report with the state called the CAR (Certified Annual Report). The CAR is filed roughly three months after the fiscal year ends, using the district's own numbers before any independent check. A separate independent audit looks at the same year later, verifies the numbers, and can correct them.

The FY24 CAR was filed in September 2024. The FY24 audit will land in summer 2026. Comparing the FY24 CAR against the four prior audited years and the year that followed reveals several numbers that do not fit — some of which paint a coherent picture of receipts being classified to the wrong fund. The audit will either confirm those numbers or correct them.

The headline number to watch

General Fund cash on hand at June 30, 2024. The FY24 CAR reports $25.91 million. The four-year audited trend points to roughly $19–20 million. The next year's internal management number is $19.3 million. The number in the FY24 CAR sits about $6 million above the trend line, with no operational story that explains the jump up or the snap back down.

That cash number is the easiest anomaly to see, but it's not the only one. Underneath the cash spike sit three more findings: the SAVE Fund spent $19 million more on capital projects than its legal funding sources allowed, the district's funds were lending each other unusual amounts of money at year-end, and the Management Fund pulled in 82% of the year's property tax growth. The K.02.01 memo from the district's own COO has already explained much of the mechanism behind them.

What "General Fund cash on hand" actually means

The General Fund is the district's main checking account — the bucket that pays teachers, runs buildings, buys textbooks, and keeps the lights on. "Cash on hand" is what's actually in the bank at the end of the fiscal year. It is the most basic measure of whether a district has the money to pay its bills.

Schools usually express this as "days of cash" — how many days the district could keep operating if no new money came in. Rating agencies and Iowa school finance experts generally consider 60 days a healthy cushion. Iowa City has been below that for years, but the trend was steady, predictable, and visible. The FY24 CAR breaks that pattern.

Underneath the cash spike: a much bigger story

The cash number is the easiest anomaly to see. But the FY24 CAR has several other line items that fit the same pattern, and together they tell a more specific story than "the General Fund had extra cash." Three findings from a line-by-line comparison of the FY23 CAR and the FY24 CAR.

Before each finding, the plain-language version. The technical detail follows for anyone who wants it.

Finding 1 — The SAVE Fund spent more than it was allowed to

The SAVE Fund is the bucket that pays for capital projects like building expansions and renovations. It's funded by a statewide one-cent sales tax and by sales-tax revenue bonds. By law, it can only spend what it has on hand from those two sources.

Plain-language version

In FY24, capital project spending out of SAVE ran $19 million past what the bond and sales tax revenue could legitimately cover. Picture a household savings account dedicated to home renovations. By household rule, you can only spend what's in that account on the renovation. In FY24, the renovation spending ran $19,000 past what the renovation savings could cover — and the books show the shortfall.

This appears on the FY24 CAR balance sheet as a negative $18,944,278 in SAVE's unassigned fund balance. A negative number in that column is unusual; the fund went from $0 a year earlier to -$18.9M.

The K.02.01 Capital Outlay Borrowing Need Memo, presented to the board on March 24, 2026 by then-COO Curt Pratt, explains the likely mechanism. The memo says property tax receipts that should have gone to the Debt Service Fund were being parked in a different fund, and capital project spending was being charged to SAVE even when the matching revenue wasn't there. The negative $19 million is what shows up on the balance sheet when that pattern continues for a full year.

Finding 2 — The district's funds were lending each other unusual amounts of money

Iowa law requires school districts to keep money in separate buckets by purpose. Think of them as separate checking accounts in a single household, each with rules about what it can pay for:

  • General Fund — operations, payroll, supplies. The big one.
  • Management Fund — liability insurance, workers' comp, unemployment, early retirement.
  • Debt Service Fund — bond principal and interest payments.
  • SAVE Fund — capital projects (funded by sales tax + bonds).
  • PPEL Fund — facility maintenance and small construction.

When one bucket pays for something that belongs to another bucket, the district records an IOU between them. These IOUs are normally tiny and get settled quickly. In FY24, they exploded.

Plain-language version

About $11 million of the Management Fund's cash sitting in the bank effectively belongs to the General Fund. The General Fund's reported cash position depends on whether those IOUs get treated as good as cash. The audit will test that.

This reframes the original cash anomaly. The FY24 CAR is not just saying "the General Fund had more cash than the trend would predict." It is saying "the General Fund's reported cash position depends on a $14 million IOU from other buckets — mostly from the Management Fund — that didn't exist at this scale a year earlier." Whether the audit accepts those IOUs decides whether the reported cash is real.

Finding 3 — Most of the property tax increase went to one bucket

Property tax growth normally gets spread across several buckets — the General Fund (operations), the Debt Service Fund (bond payments), the PPEL Fund (facilities), and the Management Fund (insurance and retirement). The split depends on the levy rates the board sets at budget time.

Plain-language version

In FY24, the district's property tax revenue grew by $10.2 million. $8.3 million of that — about 82% — went to the Management Fund alone. Only $1.7 million went to the General Fund. That kind of one-bucket concentration is unusual.

Management Fund property tax revenue went from $5.2 million in FY23 to $13.5 million in FY24 (+161%). Management Fund cash followed it: $1.14 million to $13.09 million (+1,049%).

The board can decide to raise the Management Levy, and that would be legal. The audit's Required Supplementary Information will show the certified Management Levy rate. If the rate supports that much revenue, this was a deliberate board decision to build up the Management Fund. If not, the audit reclassifies the receipts back to the buckets they belong in. Either way, this is a question with a definite answer once the audit is published.

A quick note: cash vs. IOU

Before the summary, one piece of plumbing that matters. A school district's balance sheet records two different things that both contribute to a fund's reported strength:

  • Cash & investments — actual money sitting in the bank, attributed to that bucket.
  • Interfund receivable (the IOU) — a recorded claim that another bucket owes this bucket money.

Both appear as assets on the balance sheet. But they are not the same thing. Cash is cash. An IOU is a claim — useful only if the other bucket can actually pay. When you hear about the General Fund's $25.9M cash, that is the bank balance. When you hear about the $14M interfund receivable, that is a separate line on the balance sheet for IOUs other buckets owe to the General Fund. The audit's job is to decide whether the IOUs are legitimate.

The story in one paragraph

The four anomalies above don't sit independently. They fit together into one mechanism. Money that should have gone into one set of buckets got routed into another set during the year. Capital project spending continued at the planned pace. At year-end, the books were made to balance using IOUs between the buckets. The General Fund's reported strength is higher than the four-year trend would predict because the General Fund's balance sheet now includes both $25.9 million of actual bank cash and a $14 million IOU from other buckets — both counted as assets. The SAVE Fund's $19 million shortfall is the leftover gap on the capital side: spending that ran past what the bond and sales-tax revenue legitimately funded.

What appears to have happened in FY24, step by step

  1. Property tax money from Johnson County went into the wrong buckets. Some receipts that should have gone to the General Fund and the Debt Service Fund were routed into the Management Fund instead. (That's why Management Fund property tax grew 161% while General Fund tax grew 2%.)
  2. Capital project work on FMP 2.0 and FMP 2.3 was paid out of SAVE. Bond proceeds funded most of it. But the part that should have been funded by the misclassified debt service receipts wasn't there. SAVE ended up $19 million short.
  3. At year-end, the books were balanced with IOUs between the buckets. The General Fund records itself as owed $14 million from other buckets. The Management Fund records itself as owing $11.3 million back. The IOUs roughly cover the misclassification.
  4. The General Fund's reported cash position looks healthier than the four-year trend — $25.9M reported in the CAR vs roughly $19M on trend — because the IOU from the Management Fund is being treated as if it were actual cash.

The audit's job is to test each link in this chain. If it accepts the IOUs as legitimate, the CAR mostly stands. If it reclassifies the underlying receipts back to the buckets they belong in, the FY24 CAR gets a substantial restatement.

What this is not about

One thing worth setting aside: the $10 million interfund transfer from the Health Insurance Fund to the General Fund happened in August 2025. That transfer is part of the FY26 fiscal year story and will be addressed in the FY26 audit when that lands in March 2027. It has no effect on the FY24 numbers or the FY24 audit.

This page also does not allege wrongdoing. The FY24 CAR was filed by district staff in September 2024 using the district's own accounting records as they existed at that time. The FY24 audit's job is to verify those records independently. The most common explanation for a CAR-to-audit gap is a fund-classification adjustment caught during audit fieldwork — that is exactly what audits exist to do.

The watchlist: eight things to check the moment the audit is published

The audit is expected by July 2026. When it lands, these are the eight things to compare against the FY24 state-filed report, ordered by what would tell us the most.

  1. Whether the IOUs between buckets are real The biggest single question the audit will answer.
    The state-filed FY24 report says the General Fund is owed $14.08 million from other buckets, and the Management Fund owes $11.31 million back.
    If the audit confirms those numbers, the original classification was accepted as legitimate.
    If the audit reduces them, both the General Fund's reported strength and the Management Fund's cash buildup shrink at the same time.
  2. The $19 million SAVE Fund shortfall The clearest single anomaly in the FY24 state-filed report.
    The state-filed report says SAVE spent $18.9 million more on capital projects than the bond and sales tax revenue could legitimately cover. A shortfall this size on a major capital fund is unusual. Watch whether the audit confirms it, moves prior-year numbers around to absorb it, or reclassifies revenue.
  3. The General Fund's actual bank cash The number that most directly affects the days-of-cash story above.
    The state-filed report claims $25.9 million in the General Fund bank account at June 30, 2024.
    If the audit shows $19–21 million, the misallocation theory is confirmed.
    If the audit shows $24 million or more, the year-after cash drop to $19.3 million needs its own explanation, which the district hasn't yet provided publicly.
  4. Whether the audit has to redo prior-year numbers Audits add a note when previously reported numbers were wrong.
    A note in the FY24 audit that says the prior year's General Fund, Management Fund, Debt Service Fund, or SAVE Fund balances were restated is direct confirmation that money had been put in the wrong bucket.
  5. The Management Fund's $13 million cash buildup Tests whether the 82% property tax shift was a board decision or a classification error.
    The state-filed report shows Management Fund cash at $13.09 million and property tax revenue at $13.48 million.
    The audit will show the Management Levy rate the board certified. If the rate supports that much revenue, it was a deliberate board decision. If not, the audit moves the receipts back to the buckets they belong in.
  6. Whether the Debt Service Fund's tax money was actually there The mirror image of the SAVE shortfall.
    The state-filed report shows Debt Service Fund cash at $2.16 million and property tax revenue at $12.95 million.
    If the audit moves $5–10 million of property tax receipts from the Management Fund or General Fund into Debt Service, both the SAVE shortfall and the General Fund cash overstatement get untangled at the same time.
  7. Whether the district stayed inside its legal budget The most consequential number for state oversight.
    The state-filed report has the district inside its legal spending limit by +1.67%, a cushion of about $3.3 million.
    If the audit catches unbooked bills — federal payroll tax penalties, special education deficit, construction commitments, retirement obligations — that cushion can disappear easily.
    If it flips negative, ICCSD exceeded its legal spending limit two years in a row. See the dedicated page below.
  8. Whether the auditors flag the district's accounting controls The auditors' separate report on whether the district's accounting systems are catching errors.
    The FY23 audit flagged two "significant deficiencies" and one "material weakness." If the FY24 audit adds a finding about keeping money in the right buckets, that is the institutional-level confirmation that the problem isn't a one-time mistake.

Why this matters now

The district is rebuilding its forecast, working through an audit backlog, and trying to restore the credibility that supports a future bond rating. The FY24 audit is the first independent look at the district's books in two years. A clean audit that closely matches the CAR would be a real positive signal that the underlying systems were healthier than the post-crisis reporting suggests. An audit that materially restates FY24 would confirm what the board's own internal reporting has been pointing at since the K.02.01 memo in March 2026.

Either outcome is informative. The point of this page is to make sure the right comparisons get done in plain view when the audit lands — not after months of explanation by people with reason to soften it.
