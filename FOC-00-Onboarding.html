Financial Oversight Committee · New-Member Onboarding

The District's Finances, the Committee, and Your First Year

Iowa City Community School District  ·  Prepared by Michael Parrott, community member  ·  August 2026  ·  FOC-00
The one-paragraph version Iowa City CSD is a wealthy district that ran itself out of room. Its tax base is strong ($14.6B actual valuation) and its long-term debt uses just 1.07% of that base — about a fifth of the legal limit — yet by 2026 the district was down to days of cash, borrowing from its own health-insurance fund, and unable to raise the borrowing it needed. Two unrelated problems — a debt-financed building program and a structural operating deficit — drained one shared cash pool from both sides while the board's oversight, which depended entirely on information from the office being overseen, failed to see it. This is a controls-and-oversight failure in a community that can still afford its schools, which means it is fixable. But the forward problem is now two problems, not one: the ~$6M structural deficit is joined by a projected enrollment decline worth roughly as much again in recurring revenue by 2030. The Financial Oversight Committee exists to make the recovery verifiable — and to make sure the second problem is not discovered the way the first one was.

All funds share one bank account, so both failures emptied the same pool — ending in $38.2M of unauthorized interfund loans and a hidden $10M transfer. (Page 2)

The forward gap is two problems: a ~$6M structural operating deficit that keeps base-case solvency below the board's 5% floor through FY2029, plus a projected loss of 750–815 students by 2030 worth $6.8–7.3M a year in recurring revenue. (Page 4)

1 · How it happened, 2017–2026Full narrative: “How Iowa City ran out of room”

Two unrelated failures drained one shared pool of cash

In 2016 the district looked healthy on every measure. What followed was not one bad decision but two independent problems that met in the district's bank account. Iowa law walls school funds apart on paper — sales-tax money can't pay teachers; operating money can't build gyms. But all of the district's funds share one physical bank account, so each side's spending drained the cash available to all, ledgers or no ledgers.

The capital side: a building program outran its funding

Voters approved a $191.5M bond in 2017 — the largest school bond in Iowa history, issued in three series totalling $191,525,000. With new sales-tax (SAVE) bonds of $65.8M (2022) and $71.5M (2023), total debt stacked to a $322M peak in FY2023. Construction spending kept running ahead of the revenue meant to repay it: in FY2025 alone the SAVE fund spent $55.7M against $19.1M of revenue, draining from $45.8M to $9.2M in a single year. Along the way the district paid $8.7M for an office building assessed at $5.4M. By mid-2025, total pooled cash had fallen ~42% in one year, from roughly $108M to $62M.

The operating side: a structural deficit, unwatched

Salaries and benefits are ~85% of the General Fund, and spending grew faster than flat enrollment could fund. The state's key metric — unspent budget authority — fell from 6.6% (2017) to 0.1% (2022) to −1.2% in 2023: the district had overspent its legal authority, the trigger for state-supervised recovery. Payroll ran $13.5M (9%) over budget against a planned 2–3%, much of it a roughly $19M special-education shortfall. Early-retirement obligations were paid from a levy fund that couldn't cover them, leaving that one fund owing the pool $11.4M.

Where the two failures met: borrowed funds, broken controls, and a false picture

As both sides drained, the funds began cannibalizing each other. The FY2024 audit put a number on it: $38.2M of interfund loans at mid-2024 — none with the prior board approval, repayment date, or interest Iowa law requires. In August 2025 the administration moved $10M from the health-insurance fund to cover payroll; it was not reported to the board and was omitted from the next quarterly report. It surfaced in January 2026. Behind all of it: bank reconciliations had not been done for roughly three years, monthly finances went untracked, audits ran years late, and a staff warning about “fundamental errors” in the fall of 2024 went effectively unanswered. The board's information all flowed through the office it was overseeing — and at least one report it received was false.

The 2026 reckoning — every lever compromised at once When the board learned the situation in January 2026, the normal remedies were already off the table. It approved $7.5M in cuts. It tried to borrow $25M — and every bank said no, because there were no current audits to underwrite. It paused the $104M facilities plan and sold the old headquarters for $3.2M to raise cash. The district's advisor projected roughly one week of operating cash by mid-2026 before emergency borrowing. Meanwhile $525,110 in federal late-filing penalties (Sept 2023–June 2025) and a since-lifted federal tax lien had accrued, and the credit rating had been withdrawn back in October 2024.

2 · Where the district standsFY24 audit · peer benchmark · FY25 open questions

Clean books, broken controls — and the thinnest cushion in the peer group

The FY24 audit (delivered June 10, 2026): what it did and didn't find

The good news is real. No missing money. The financial statements earned an unmodified (“clean”) opinion and no going-concern warning. The two scariest self-reported numbers got better under audit: the district's own filing had shown a $19M hole in the SAVE construction fund — a bookkeeping error; the fund actually ended +$14.7M. General Fund cash was overstated ~$3.4M (real figure $22.5M), but nothing vanished.

The bad news is about how money was handled. Five material weaknesses in core controls — payroll, disbursements, receipts, bank reconciliations, year-end close (eight including federal-program findings). $38M moved between funds without required board approval. Spending exceeded the legal capital budget by $19.5M even after a $24.2M mid-year amendment. Federal opinions were qualified on two program clusters (unallowable ESSER costs; missing documentation), costing the district its low-risk-auditee status. Several findings are repeats from FY23.

Peer context: the severity is the outlier, not the existence of findings Across Iowa's 15 large districts, 91% of the last decade's audit-years had zero material weaknesses, and no district had ever carried more than two at once. ICCSD's FY24 carried five (eight with federal). Only one peer — Davenport — has drawn a qualified federal opinion in that span, and it has since worked its findings back to zero. Davenport is the useful precedent: a large Iowa district that was somewhere similar and recovered.

One distinction worth getting right: FY23 and FY24 broke different rules

FY23 violated General Fund spending authority (Iowa Code ch. 257; UAB of −1.2%) — the violation that involves the state's School Budget Review Committee. FY24 violated the certified budget by function (ch. 24): instruction and support services actually came in under budget; the entire $19.5M breach was in capital/construction. “Two years over budget” is technically true but conflates two different laws, two different causes, and two different fixes.

FY25 is the open question — watch the audit due this fall

The FY25 books were kept under the same practices the FY24 audit flagged, and the district's own unaudited FY25 state filing shows the same fingerprints. These are questions, not findings — but they are the right questions for the committee to carry into audit season:

  • The Student Activity Fund was brought to exactly $0.00 at year-end, balanced by a $975K IOU to the General Fund — is that an interfund loan requiring board approval, and how will the auditor treat it?
  • ~$2.8M of prior-year corrections flow both directions through the filing (vs. $0 the prior year), including a suspiciously round $1,111,050 adjustment in the self-insurance fund. What are they?
  • FY25 opened the General Fund ~$314K above the audited FY24 closing balance. When is the correction booked?

3 · The structural gapForecast base case = PFM (district's own advisor)

Two gaps, not one — and the second one grows

The forecast below uses PFM's own seven-year model as the base case — not the printed budget, which implied a ~$6M surplus where PFM projects ~$225M of spending against a $212M budget: a ~$6M operating deficit. That correction pulls the whole path down. Then a second, slower force arrives underneath it: enrollment is now projected to fall, and in Iowa funding follows the student.

General Fund solvency ratio, FY2020–FY2029
Reserves as % of revenue · solid = audited · open marker = unaudited estimate · dashed = projection, shaded band = conservative↔optimistic · board target 10–15%, floor 5%

Audited FY20–24: 4.2%, 6.4%, 2.8%, 2.5%, 8.1%. FY25 shown at the model estimate (4.1%); the district's own unaudited estimate is ~6%. Projections FY26–29 (base): 0.5%, 1.2%, 2.4%, 3.5% — below the 5% floor every year through FY2029, even before the conservative band. The FY24 rebound was driven by an unusually low spend year and the cash-reserve levy; it did not hold.

The second gap: enrollment is projected to decline, and every forecast now agrees

Enrollment was roughly flat for five years — 14,572 at its FY21 peak, 14,379 in FY25 — which is why it has not featured in the crisis story. That period is over. The district's own demographer reversed a decade of growth projections in February 2025, and three independent methods now converge on the mid-13,000s by 2030.

Why this belongs in a financial memo, not a demographic one At roughly $9,000 per student, a loss of 750–815 students is a $6.8–7.3M recurring annual revenue headwind by 2030 — about $18M cumulative over 2026–2030, the same order of magnitude as the ~$6M operating deficit. The forward hole is not ~$6M a year; it is ~$6M now, trending toward ~$13M by 2030 unless recurring costs come down by a similar amount. Iowa's formula smooths the hit via prior-year enrollment, a budget guarantee and a declining-enrollment supplement — which makes it easier to under-react to.

What is actually driving it

Births, overwhelmingly. Johnson County births peaked at 1,856 in 2016 and fell to ~1,265 by 2024; kindergarten has fallen four years running (1,035→987). That pipeline is already fixed for this decade — those children are born.

Not vouchers — which matters, because the public narrative says otherwise. Of ~1,440 ESA users, roughly 78% were already in private school; the defensible public→private transfer is about 175 students.

Two offsetting forces: immigration masked the decline (1,390 in FY25, easing to 1,052 in FY26), while building permits argue for stabilization — supporting the baseline over the pessimistic case.

The solvency path above is probably optimistic The model underlying it assumes enrollment declines ~0.3%/yr through FY31. The demographer evidence implies roughly 1.1%/yr, and the model's own risk register flags “run a scenario at 1–2% annual decline” as work not yet done. That scenario has never been run, and it is the one the evidence supports — a reasonable early request from the committee.

Read everything FY2024-forward as unaudited: the fund-balance anchor carries ~±$3M of slack (solvency ±1.5 points), and the 1725 N. Dodge sale is excluded from all base lines — the biggest unmodeled upside.

4 · Cash and borrowing capacityThe warrant treadmill · through June 2027

Liquidity is being managed; solvency is not

The gap above is a solvency problem — the district spends more than it takes in. What the district has been managing is something different and more urgent: liquidity, the question of whether there is cash in the account on payroll day. Those are not the same problem, and the tools that fix one do not fix the other.

General Fund month-end cash, April 2026 – June 2027 ($M, with $25M warrant and interfund flows)
Base case line · band = conservative↔optimistic · property taxes arrive in two waves (October, April)

On operating cash alone the General Fund goes negative in September 2026 (−$0.4M base); the $25M warrant bridges that. But repayment (~$26.5M) moves the real low to June 2027: $0.4M base, −$7.2M conservative — and PFM's own June 30, 2027 estimate (~$9.95M ≈ 16 days) would not cover July 2027 payroll (~$11.8M). A second ~$10M warrant is already planned for FY2027. Note the March 2026 starting balance itself included a $10M loan from the health-insurance fund.

What the warrant does and doesn't do

A revenue anticipation warrant is short-term borrowing against taxes not yet received. It moves cash within a year; it adds no resources and must be repaid the same cycle. The $25M warrant does its job — it gets the district through the autumn trough. But repayment lands in spring 2027, which is why the genuine low point is June 2027, not September 2026, and why a second warrant is already planned.

Note also that the “healthy” $13.1M March 2026 balance was itself propped up by the $10M loan from the health-insurance fund — the transaction that started the crisis.

Market access is a matter of scale, not a binary. The district did place $3.0M of taxable anticipatory warrants on March 10, 2026 with a local bank; it was the $25M ask seven weeks later that found no taker.

The distinction to hold onto Warrants and interfund loans manage liquidity. Only closing the structural gap fixes solvency. A district can borrow its way through every cash trough and still be insolvent on an operating basis — and the borrowing itself gets more expensive and harder to arrange each year, as the refused $25M bank bid showed. Watch for the moment when a liquidity fix is presented as if it were a solvency fix.

Debt structure — capacity is not the problem

  • GO debt: $141.25M outstanding at 6/30/25, of $191,525,000 issued across three series. At 1.07% of the $14.62B valuation, that is roughly a fifth of the 5% statutory limit.
  • SAVE (sales-tax) debt: $146.93M outstanding, payable “solely from the sales taxes received by the District” — a revenue pledge, not a claim on the General Fund.
  • Coverage on the SAVE pledge has run 1.25–1.62× from FY2018 through FY2026 and has never approached 1.00×. The construction fund's drawdown from $45.8M to $9.2M is a capital-program problem — nothing left to build with — not, on the evidence available, a bondholder security problem.

So the borrowing constraint is not debt capacity. It is that no one will underwrite a borrower without current audited financials — which is exactly why the $25M bank request failed in April 2026, and why PFM concludes no capital borrowing is responsible before spring 2028, gating ~$18.5M of planned PPEL work.

5 · Rating and disclosureMoody's actions · SEC Rule 15c2-12

Top-rated when it borrowed; unrated seven years later

The district's credit history is the clearest outside verdict on what happened — and the agency put its warning in writing well before the crisis broke.

Moody's rating history, 2017–2028
Issuer / GO unlimited tax rating · solid = actual rating actions · dashed = hypothetical future application, not an agency view

Sources: Series 2017C, 2019 and 2020 Official Statements (rating at issuance); Moody's rating actions of 23 Mar 2021, 14 Jun 2023 and 9 Oct 2024, the last with its List of Affected Credit Ratings confirming ICCSD's prior rating as Aa3 across the issuer rating and all four GO series. The 2028 point is this project's own model output (central estimate Baa1/Baa2, ~20% chance of no rating) — not a Moody's opinion.

The warning, in the agency's own words

Moody's, 14 June 2023 — affirming Aa3, assigning a negative outlook “The negative outlook reflects the district's trend of declining fund balance and cash. If reserves do not begin to meaningfully improve, the rating will likely move downward.”

The force of this is easy to miss. The March 2021 action states that Moody's “does not usually assign outlooks to local governments with this amount of debt.” In June 2023 it assigned one anyway, and made it negative. That was a deliberate signal — 16 months before the withdrawal, naming precisely the deterioration the FY24 audit and the 2026 cash crisis went on to confirm.

Two honest qualifications. The 2021 two-notch cut concluded a sector-wide methodology review Moody's opened that January, so it is not purely district-specific — though even then the rationale cited the district's “narrow financial position.” And the 2024 withdrawal was a batch action covering 61 local-government issuers (~$3.7B of debt) whose reviews had been opened on 27 June 2024: ICCSD was not singled out. What put it in that group is not in doubt, though — Moody's states it withdrew because it had “insufficient or otherwise inadequate information to support the maintenance of the rating.” For a school district, that means the audits.

Worth noting too that the June 2023 action counted “growing enrollment” as a credit strength — a premise since reversed (page 4).

Continuing disclosure — stated precisely

Because the district has public bonds, it signed undertakings under SEC Rule 15c2-12. This is the one part of the compliance picture where the counterparty is federal securities regulation rather than a state agency, so it is worth getting exactly right.

What is required Annual financial information within 210 days of year end — January 26. Notice of listed events within 10 business days; a rating change is listed with no materiality qualifier. Breach is expressly not a bond default — the sole remedy is an action to compel performance.

On the record, the district filed on time. All five annual filings FY2021–FY2025 met the deadline, and the undertaking expressly permits unaudited statements at the deadline with audited ones to follow “when they become available.” There is therefore no clean claim that the late audits breached the Rule — worth knowing before anyone asserts otherwise in a public meeting.

What did happen is narrower but real. The FY2023 filing promised the audited report “within 90 days.” It arrived ~16 months late, and in FY2024–25 the language quietly became “as soon as it is available.” A promise to the market, made and then dropped, is fair to ask about.

Open, not established Was a material-event notice filed for the October 2024 withdrawal, and when? The district clearly can file them — it posted one within three days for a $3M warrant in March 2026 — which sharpens the question. What do the 2022/2023 SAVE undertakings require? Did S&P, which rated the SAVE debt A+, act separately? None is an allegation; all three are unanswered.

6 · The committeeCharter adopted May 26, 2026 (Policy 200.3G4)

A real audit committee, built from a failed one — with its habits still to be formed

What changed in the May 2026 charter

ScopeGeneral advisoryEight areas: statutory claims & reporting (Iowa Code 279.29, 291.7–.8); reporting & transparency; budget & planning; audit oversight (incl. auditor selection, RFP every 5 years); internal controls & risk; compliance & ethics (incl. fraud complaints); policy review; self-assessment
Terms—3-year staggered terms, max two; first cohort assigned 1-, 2-, and 3-year terms by random draw

Ground rules every member operates under

  • Advisory only. The committee recommends; all final authority rests with the board. Committees cannot legally obligate the district.
  • Iowa open-meetings law applies. Agendas posted 24 hours ahead; minutes are public record; new members must complete IPIB-approved open-meetings/open-records training. Closed sessions only on narrow Iowa Code 21.5 grounds.
  • Attendance is enforced: members who miss more than 25% of meetings can be put up for removal.
  • Workload is heaviest now, by design. The charter's bi-weekly claims duty implies ~26 cycles/year on top of formal meetings and audit-season work. Expect the recovery period to be the peak.
Settle early, with counsel Iowa Code 279.29 permits delegating the claims audit to designated board members or a CPA — not obviously to community volunteers. The committee's review can be a working layer, with formal allowance resting with the board or its designees. Clarify before the first claims cycle.

Why independence is the design problem

The core failure of 2023–2026 was not missing reports — it was that every report the board saw flowed through the office being overseen, and at least one was false. A committee that reads only management-prepared summaries inherits that exposure. The practical antidotes, all legal under Ch. 21: standing access to source data (check register, fund-balance detail, bank statements obtained directly); auditor sessions without management present; sub-quorum contact between the chair and the auditor or any concerned employee, without CFO/superintendent routing; and periodic reconciliation of reported balances against actual bank statements.

The charter's inversion — know where the leverage is The charter's most prescriptive requirement (bi-weekly claims review) guards against a failure the district did not have — nobody stole anything. The failures it did have (unauthorized transfers, false reporting, untracked finances, late audits, a structural deficit) sit under its most general language. Specifying those controls — metrics, limits, escalation, independent verification — is the committee's first real job.

Don't confuse the FOC with the state's School Budget Review Committee (SBRC), which the district appeared before in June 2026 — press coverage sometimes calls both “financial oversight.”

7 · The calendarStatutory cycle · live district obligations

The year has a legal shape — most of what went wrong was a missed date

Nearly every failure in this story is a deadline that passed: audits filed years late, a certified budget exceeded, interfund loans never repaid by October 1. A committee that knows the calendar can see trouble coming, because the calendar is the same every year.

Live obligations the committee inherits

  • Monthly financial reports to the board, the auditor, and the SBRC — required by SBRC vote, June 9, 2026.
  • Return appearance at the SBRC's October 2026 meeting.
  • FY25 audit — and note the three different dates on record: the CFO's plan says November 2026, PFM says “no later than December 31, 2026,” and the audit firm's outer bound is “no later than May 2027.” Worth tracking as one item with three answers.
  • FY26 audit by March 31, 2027 — which would make the district current again. (The district's own action plan writes “March 30”; the statutory date is the 31st.)
  • FY23 restatement in progress; forensic audit committed after the FY24–25 audits; State Auditor reaudit requested under §11.6(4).

CAR compliance, for context: FY2024 was filed Jan 22, 2025 and FY2025 on Nov 20, 2025 — against a Sept 15 deadline, so both were late, but the trend is sharply improving. Dates are read from the DE form stamps.

The SBRC, and why it is not a formality The state School Budget Review Committee has statutory power over spending authority. ICCSD's June 9, 2026 appearance was triggered by the categorical-balance test, not simply negative unspent authority: a regular-program balance of −$22,958,507, less the $18,373,455 special-education deficit MSA, leaving −$4,585,052 on the appearance test.

Two things to carry from that. First, an MSA grants spending authority, not cash — the revenue arrives roughly a full fiscal year later, which the CFO has described as “a three-year process to get back to whole.” Requests run on a fall and spring cycle, processed as a class action across districts.

Second, and more pointedly: a January 8, 2025 SBRC hearing had already directed the district to file monthly financial reports — and they were never provided. The June 2026 order repeats that requirement. Whether it is now actually being met is a reasonable standing question for this committee.

For scale: the district's finances went to the State Board of Education on June 18, 2026 — the only body that can move toward a state takeover.

8 · What to watch, and what to askMetrics · first-meeting questions

Five or six numbers, watched relentlessly, would have caught all of it

Definition of done

Oversight intensity should taper on evidence, never on the calendar: tight now (monthly cadence, weekly cash + 13-week forecast, low claims thresholds), stepping down only as binary, verifiable exit criteria are met — loans repaid and independently confirmed, consecutive on-time closes, findings resolved or tracked, metrics out of red for two quarters. “Recovered” means sustaining the targets for a full fiscal year, not a good month.

Ten questions worth asking in your first meetings

  1. Are all interfund loans now board-approved in advance with repayment dates and interest, as Iowa law requires — and what is the current balance, by fund?
  2. What are the $25M warrant's terms, the spring 2027 repayment plan, and the trigger for the planned second ~$10M warrant?
  3. Which FY24 corrective actions are complete, which are past due, and who owns each? (Ask for the tracker, not a summary.)
  4. Will the FY25 auditor treat the Activity Fund's $975K year-end IOU to the General Fund as an interfund loan requiring board approval?
  5. What is the $1,111,050 prior-year adjustment in the self-insurance fund, and the rest of the ~$2.8M of FY25 corrections?
  6. What is the repayment exposure on the two qualified federal (ESSER) program findings?
  7. Does the committee have standing, direct access to bank statements — and who reconciles reported balances against them?
  8. What is counsel's position on Iowa Code 279.29 — can community members perform claims review, or must allowance rest with board designees?
  9. Is there a documented, anonymous channel for staff to raise financial concerns to the committee — the early warning missing in 2024?
  10. What, precisely, is the public definition of “recovered” — which metrics, what targets, sustained how long?
  11. What enrollment assumption underlies the multi-year projections, and has anyone run the 1–2%/yr decline scenario the demographer evidence supports?
  12. Was a material-event notice filed for the October 2024 rating withdrawal, and on what date?
  13. Are the monthly reports the SBRC ordered in June 2026 actually going to the board, the auditor, and the SBRC — every month?
  14. Which FY25 audit date is real: the CFO's November 2026, PFM's December 31, or the audit firm's May 2027?

The reference page

Iowa school-finance glossary

Spending authority / Unspent Authorized Budget (UAB)
Iowa caps what a district may spend each year (ch. 257), separate from cash held. UAB is the unused room under that cap; negative UAB is unlawful and brings in the SBRC. The most-watched Iowa health metric.
Solvency ratio
Assigned + unassigned GF balance ÷ GF revenue (less AEA flow-through). Target 10–15%, floor 5%.
Days cash on hand
GF cash ÷ average daily spending; GFOA guideline ~60. (The district's internal “Day's Net Cash” KPI, target 90–120, is a different formula.)
CAR vs. ACFR
The CAR is the district's own unaudited state filing, due months before the ACFR (audited report). FY23's CAR overstated the GF balance by $844K (8.3%) — the largest gap among large districts. Treat CAR figures as provisional.
Aid & Levy
The worksheet setting each district's funding: per-pupil state aid + local levies. Funding follows certified enrollment.
SAVE
Statewide penny sales tax for school infrastructure. Capital only — cannot pay operating costs.
PPEL & Management Levy
Levies restricted to facilities/equipment (PPEL) and insurance/early-retirement costs (Management). Not operating money.
Cash reserve levy
Raises cash (not spending authority) to rebuild GF reserves — the tool behind the FY24 solvency rebound.
SBRC
State School Budget Review Committee — statutory power over spending authority. Negative UAB lands a district on its docket. Not the FOC.
Revenue anticipation warrant
Short-term borrowing against taxes not yet received. Repaid the same cycle — adds no resources.
Material weakness
The most serious control flag short of a misstatement: a gap that could let a material error or fraud go uncaught.
Single audit / low-risk auditee
The federal-grant audit layered on the financial audit. Clean history earns lighter testing; ICCSD lost it in FY24.
MSA (Modified Supplemental Amount)
Extra spending authority from the SBRC for defined causes — special-ed deficits, dropout prevention, English learners, enrollment growth. Grants authority, not cash; revenue follows about a year later.
SEC Rule 15c2-12 / continuing disclosure
The undertaking a bond issuer signs to file annual financial information (here within 210 days) and notice “listed events” within 10 business days. Filed to EMMA, the MSRB's public repository.
Listed event
One of 16 occurrences requiring prompt notice to bondholders. A rating change is listed with no materiality qualifier — reportable regardless of judgement.
GOULT
General obligation, unlimited tax — backed by full faith and credit with authority to levy property taxes without limit as to rate or amount. The strongest municipal security form.
Debt service coverage
Pledged revenue ÷ maximum annual debt service. ICCSD's SAVE coverage runs 1.25–1.62×; below 1.00× the pledge would not cover the payment.
