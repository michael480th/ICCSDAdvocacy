# ICCSD Financial Oversight Committee — Operating Design Principles

*Input on how the committee should run, grounded in the adopted charter and the district's documented failures. Prepared June 2026.*

The charter defines what the committee is responsible for, not how the work gets done. These design choices will determine whether the committee provides real oversight or becomes a paperwork ritual. They fall into a natural sequence: independence first, because nothing else works without it; a limits framework, because prevention beats correction; a phased intensity model, because the district is in a fire drill today and should not be governed forever as if it were; and then the review processes, mechanics, and reporting that carry the daily work.

---

## 1. Independence and access to information

The core failure was not missing reports — it was that the board's information all flowed through the office it was supposed to oversee, and at least one report was false. The committee inherits that exposure unless it builds in independence from the start.

- **Independent sessions, structured within Iowa's open meetings law.** Corporate-style closed "executive sessions" do not transfer: as a board committee, the FOC may close a session only for the narrow reasons in Iowa Code 21.5 (confidential records, litigation strategy with counsel, certain personnel evaluations), by recorded two-thirds vote. Three lawful tools achieve the same independence: noticed open meetings at which management is not a participant (calendared with the auditor at minimum annually and at audit delivery); sub-quorum contact — the chair or any group below a quorum may meet or speak with the auditor privately at any time, since without a quorum there is no "meeting" under Chapter 21, with substance brought back to a noticed meeting; and a genuine closed session where a 21.5 ground applies. When these are routine, none of them is a signal.
- **Source data, independently verified.** Standing read access to the full check register export, fund balance detail, and bank/investment statements obtained directly — and periodic reconciliation of reported fund balances against those statements. This is the specific control against falsified reporting; a committee that sees only management-prepared summaries has no defense against it.
- **Direct access without management routing.** The independent auditor, and any district employee with a financial concern, may contact the FOC chair directly — without prior knowledge or approval of the CFO or superintendent. One-on-one communication with the chair is not a meeting under Chapter 21, so this channel is fully permissible.
- **Chair owns the agenda.** The charter has the CFO facilitating meetings and the chair responsible for agendas and follow-up. In practice: the community-member chair sets the agenda and runs the meeting; the CFO supports and presents. The overseen party should not direct the oversight process.

## 2. Board-approved financial limits with defined escalation

The single most important preventive structure available: the board adopts a short list of key financial metrics, each with a normal operating range for management and a hard outer limit set by the board. The consequences of crossing each line are written into policy in advance, so a breach triggers a defined response rather than a debate about whether the situation is serious. This is standard practice at large financial institutions and scales down to a school district readily.

**How the zones work, using days cash on hand as the example:**

| Zone | Days cash on hand | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Green — management operating range | 50–75 | Normal operations. Reported on the standing dashboard. |
| Yellow — management trigger breached | 40–50 or 75–90 | Management presents a written action plan to the FOC within 30 days, with a named owner and timeline. The FOC tracks progress at every meeting until the metric returns to green. |
| Red — board limit breached | Below 40 or above 90 | Pre-committed actions take effect automatically: for example, a hiring freeze, a freeze on non-essential spending, a board-approved recovery plan within 60 days, and weekly cash reporting until resolved. |

Two design principles make this work: consequences are decided before any breach occurs, while everyone is calm; and the ranges have an upper bound as well as a lower one — sustained excess cash signals over-taxing or deferred investment, which keeps the framework balanced rather than punitive.

**Candidate metrics (five or six, no more):**

- **Days cash on hand.** The example above.
- **Unspent budget authority.** Iowa districts are legally constrained by spending authority, not only cash; a district can hold cash and still illegally exceed its authority. The state's School Budget Review Committee becomes involved when authority goes negative, so the board's red line should sit comfortably above zero.
- **Solvency ratio** (unassigned general fund balance as a percentage of revenues). The measure Iowa's Department of Management publishes and the rating agencies watch — directly relevant to restoring the district's credit rating.
- **Actual vs. budgeted FTEs.** The position-control metric. A tolerance of roughly one percent would have surfaced the payroll overrun within months rather than at year end.
- **Year-to-date spending vs. budget, by major category.** Variance beyond a set percentage triggers yellow.
- **Interfund borrowing.** Zero tolerance: any interfund loan without prior board approval is automatically red. The event that started the crisis becomes a permanent tripwire.

**Governance mechanics.** The FOC is advisory and cannot set limits itself, but its charter authorizes it to recommend financial policies to the board — that is the vehicle. The board adopts the limits as policy; the FOC reviews them annually during budget development (Resp. 3), shows every metric against its zones on each dashboard, and ensures any breach, exception, or limit change goes to the board in open session.

## 3. Phased oversight: start tight, taper on evidence

By any honest reading of the limits above, the district is in the red zone today — the recovery plan *is* the red-zone response, already in progress. Oversight intensity should match that reality now and step down as conditions are verifiably restored. This is how oversight is structured everywhere it follows a failure — regulatory consent orders, material-weakness remediation, distressed-company turnarounds — and the principle is always the same: intensity tapers against evidence, never against the calendar.

| Phase | Cadence and intensity |
|---|---|
| **1 — Recovery** (now, until exit criteria met) | Committee meets monthly. Weekly cash position with a 13-week rolling forecast. Claims review with low exception thresholds. All interfund activity requires prior board approval. Monthly FTE report against budget. Close timeliness tracked from month one. Setup work completed in this phase: limits adopted, reporting channel stood up, corrective-action tracker built. |
| **2 — Stabilization** | Committee meets every other month. Cash position bi-weekly; 13-week forecast refreshed monthly. Exception thresholds widen. |
| **3 — Steady state** | The charter's baseline: six-plus meetings aligned to the financial and audit calendar, dashboard cadence, annual depth on budget and audit. |

**Exit criteria are the whole game.** Movement between phases should require objectively verifiable conditions — for example: the interfund loan fully repaid and independently confirmed against bank statements; a defined number of consecutive months of on-time monthly close; an audit cycle with material weaknesses resolved or on a tracked plan; all board-limit metrics in green or yellow for two consecutive quarters; no overdue items on the corrective-action tracker. Each criterion is binary; none depends on whether things "feel better." The committee certifies the criteria are met; the board approves each phase change in open session.

The phased structure answers both audiences' core fear at once. To the administration: the heightened scrutiny has a defined end, and management controls the pace by hitting milestones. To the board and public: oversight cannot quietly fade as attention moves on, because relaxation requires proof.

## 4. Proportionate, risk-based review (especially bi-weekly claims)

The bi-weekly claims process under Iowa Code 279.29 is the heaviest recurring duty in the charter. Volunteers cannot and should not read every line; the process should direct attention where risk lives.

- **Review by exception.** Full check register available every cycle; reviewer attention directed by defined flags — payments above a dollar threshold, new or changed vendors, off-cycle or manual checks, anything touching interfund activity, and lines tied to over-budget accounts. Thresholds sit low in Phase 1 and widen with each phase.
- **Rotating reviewer, full-committee ratification.** A single member or sub-quorum group handles each bi-weekly cycle against the exception list; the full committee ratifies at formal meetings. This also respects Iowa open meetings law, since a quorum convening bi-weekly would itself constitute a public meeting.
- **Clarify what legally counts as "allowance."** Iowa Code 279.29 permits delegation of the claims audit to designated board members or a CPA — not community volunteers. The committee's review is a working layer; formal allowance rests with the board or its designated members (plausibly the board liaisons). Settle this with counsel before the first cycle.
- **Make the bi-weekly report carry context.** The charter lets the committee specify report contents, and numbers without comparators catch nothing. Each report should show: fund balances with change from prior period; interfund activity as its own called-out line; payroll run totals vs. the same run prior year and vs. budget phasing; and year-to-date payroll against budget.

## 5. Controls matched to the failures that actually happened

Each documented failure has a specific control. The committee's Phase 1 work plan: verify each exists, and recommend it where it doesn't.

| Failure | Control that catches it | Charter home |
|---|---|---|
| $10M undisclosed interfund transfer | Bi-weekly fund balance reporting with period-over-period change; interfund activity flagged as its own line | Resp. 1 (291.7 reporting) |
| False quarterly report (loan shown as repaid) | Independent reconciliation to bank/investment statements; private auditor sessions | Resp. 2, 4; Authority section |
| Payroll 9% over budget | Position control (no hire without an approved, funded position number); monthly payroll budget-vs-actual with FTE counts | Resp. 3, 5 |
| Years of untracked monthly finances | Monthly close calendar with deadlines; bank reconciliations evidenced monthly; close timeliness tracked as a standing metric | Resp. 2, 5 |
| Late audits; FY23 material weaknesses never corrected | Corrective-action tracker with named owners and due dates, reviewed every meeting until cleared | Resp. 4 |
| Structural deficit; enrollment decline; lost credit rating | Multi-year projections on a defined cadence; 13-week rolling cash forecast during the recovery period | Resp. 3 |

Note the inversion: the charter's most prescriptive requirement (claims review) guards against a failure the district did not have, while the failures it did have are covered only by its most general language. Specification is the committee's first job.

## 6. Practices adapted from large-institution oversight committees

The committee charters of major regulated financial institutions are far heavier than a district needs, but several core provisions transfer directly, beyond the access provisions in Section 1.

- **The committee owns the auditor relationship.** Two additions complete what the charter started: an annual written confirmation of independence from the auditor, listing all relationships with the district; and an explicit committee role in resolving any disagreement between management and the auditor — precisely the moment oversight either functions or does not.
- **Authority to engage outside expertise.** The committee may request board approval to retain outside advisors, with a modest annual amount budgeted. Without this, a committee that suspects a problem depends on the very staff it is questioning to investigate it.
- **Quorum and voting.** A majority of appointed members constitutes a quorum; matters are determined by majority vote of members present. Absent from the charter; adoptable as an operating procedure immediately.
- **Fraud prevention as an affirmative duty.** Oversee management's development and implementation of fraud detection and prevention measures — not merely review complaints after they arrive.

## 7. Workload realism and committee mechanics

- **Reconcile the cadence math.** Roughly 26 bi-weekly claims cycles per year against the formal meeting schedule of whichever phase applies. The between-meeting process in Section 4 must be adopted explicitly at the outset, or the committee will be out of compliance with its own charter within months.
- **Adopt what the charter omits.** No conflict-of-interest disclosure, no quorum definition, no voting rules. Adopt these as operating procedures in Phase 1 and recommend charter incorporation through the built-in charter review (Resp. 8).
- **Honest time expectations.** Formal meetings plus a rotating bi-weekly review duty plus audit-season work — heaviest in Phase 1, by design. Stating this up front protects the 75% attendance standard from becoming the committee's first crisis.

## 8. Reporting and culture

- **Report to the board in plain language.** Board updates should be a one-page dashboard — cash position, budget vs. actual, close timeliness, open corrective actions, each metric against its zones, current phase and progress toward exit criteria — not a recitation of activity.
- **Stand up the complaint channel.** Responsibility 6 directs the committee to review procedures for complaints about accounting matters, internal controls, and suspected fraud. This is the institutional home for an anonymous staff reporting channel: a documented procedure for the submission, receipt, retention, and treatment of such complaints — including confidential, anonymous complaints by employees — with the committee overseeing resolution. It belongs among the committee's first agenda items; this was the early-warning mechanism missing when staff-level knowledge never reached the board.
- **Tone: help the recovery succeed.** The committee's value is forward-looking. Frame the work plan around making the recovery verifiable — not relitigating fault.

*Prepared as constructive input on committee operations. The objective throughout: a committee that makes the district's recovery verifiable — oversight that starts tight and tapers on evidence, limits set in advance, information that flows independently, and review processes sized so volunteer members can sustain them.*
