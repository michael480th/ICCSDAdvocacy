ICCSD · FY24 Budget Watch

The FY24 budget cushion is $3.3 million wide. Here's what could close it.

In FY23, Iowa City Community School District spent more than its legal budget. That's what brought the district before the state and started the chain of events that defined the 2026 financial crisis. The FY24 report the district has filed with the state puts the district back inside the limit, but only by $3.3 million. This page walks through four things the audit might find that would close that cushion — and what a second straight year of overspending would mean.

What "spending authority" actually means

Iowa law gives every school district a legal spending limit for each fiscal year. The limit is calculated from a formula that uses enrollment, state aid, and the district's tax base. A district cannot legally spend more than its limit, even if it has the cash to do so. Exceeding the limit triggers state oversight.

School finance professionals usually talk about this as the UAB (Unspent Authorized Budget) — the gap between what the district is allowed to spend and what it actually spent, reported as a percentage of expenditures.

  • Positive UAB% means the district spent less than it was allowed. Healthy.
  • Negative UAB% means the district spent more than it was allowed. This is a violation of Iowa law and triggers state oversight.

Iowa City Community School District's FY23 audited UAB% was −1.20%. That negative number is the violation that brought the district before the state's School Budget Review Committee and started the chain of events that has defined the 2026 financial crisis.

What the FY24 state-filed report claims

The FY24 state-filed report (CAR) puts the district back inside its legal limit, with a UAB% of roughly +1.67% against General Fund expenditures of $198 million. In dollar terms, the district reportedly spent about $3.31 million less than its legal limit.

$3.3 million is thin

$3.31 million sounds like a lot. Against a $198 million budget, it's not. It's the difference between "stayed inside the limit" and "exceeded the limit" by less than a single payroll cycle.

When the FY24 audit lands, the auditors will look for bills the district owed at June 30, 2024 but hadn't yet recorded as expenses. Every bill they add becomes an expense for FY24, and shrinks the cushion.

Here are four kinds of bills that the audit could find — each one already documented in board materials or press reporting.

The math: any two of these in combination closes the cushion.

Each one of the items above is already documented somewhere in the public record. The cushion is $3.31 million. Federal payroll tax penalties alone is $175,000 (5% of the cushion). The construction bills alone could close 75% of it. Add any two and the cushion goes through zero.

What a flip would mean

If the FY24 audit shows a negative UAB%, ICCSD will have exceeded its legal spending limit in two consecutive years — FY23 (already audited) and FY24. That has real consequences beyond a number on a page.

State oversight extends backward

The district has already appeared before the state's School Budget Review Committee in June 2026. This is the state body that watches districts that exceed their limits. A FY24 violation in addition to FY23 means the oversight clock started earlier than the public timeline currently assumes, and the district's pending request for extra spending authority becomes harder for the state to approve cleanly.

State takeover discussion becomes more serious

Public reporting since spring 2026 has noted that, in the worst case, the state could move to take over the district. That outcome has always required a pattern, not a single bad year. Two consecutive years of violation is exactly the kind of pattern that would justify escalation.

The bond rating recovery gets harder

S&P, Moody's, and Fitch all treat multi-year budget violations as a serious credit concern. The district's stated goal is to get a public bond rating again by 2028. A FY24 violation pushes that timeline back and makes the conversation with rating agencies much harder.

The district has to formally tell its bondholders

The district has agreements with the people who hold its existing bonds to keep them informed of material financial events. A two-year spending violation is exactly the kind of event those agreements require disclosure of, on a specific timeline, with specific language. This is paperwork the district has to file regardless of how the audit is framed publicly.

What this is not

This is not a prediction. The FY24 audit could land close to the state-filed report's +1.67%. It could land slightly below. It could flip negative. The point of this page is to note that the cushion is thin enough that any of those outcomes is plausible, and that the public should understand what each outcome would mean.

This is also not an allegation of wrongdoing. The FY24 state-filed report was prepared by district staff in September 2024 using the records as they existed at that time. The audit's job is to verify those records independently. The most common reason audits move a number is that a bill was owed at year-end but not yet recorded — that is normal audit work, not misconduct.

What to watch when the audit lands

The FY24 audit is a long document, and most of it is technical. Four specific pieces are where the answer to the question on this page will show up.

  1. The budget-versus-actual comparison. Every audit includes a separate table that shows what the district was allowed to spend and what it actually spent. This is where the audited UAB% appears.
  2. The auditors' list of problems they found. If the auditors flag "spending authority" or "compliance with budget" as a problem, that is the direct signal.
  3. The audit's footnotes on bills the district owes. Pay attention to the footnotes on construction contracts, special education, and early retirement. If those numbers are noticeably different from prior years, the audit caught something.
  4. The auditors' separate report on the district's accounting controls. A finding here about how the district tracks spending against its legal limit is the institutional-level confirmation that this isn't a one-time accounting error.
