ICCSD · FY24 Audit Watchlist

When the FY24 audit comes out, here's what to check first

The Iowa City Community School District's FY24 audit is expected in July 2026. It will be the first independent look at the district's books since the financial crisis came to light. The version of FY24 that the district has already filed with the state has several numbers that don't fit the pattern of the four years before it — or the year that followed. This page walks through what to look for when the audit comes out, and why each piece matters.

The two-minute version

Every Iowa school district files an annual financial report with the state called the CAR (Certified Annual Report). The CAR is filed roughly three months after the fiscal year ends, using the district's own numbers before any independent check. A separate independent audit looks at the same year later, verifies the numbers, and can correct them.

The FY24 CAR was filed in September 2024. The FY24 audit will land in summer 2026. Comparing the FY24 CAR against the four prior audited years and the year that followed reveals several numbers that do not fit — some of which paint a coherent picture of receipts being classified to the wrong fund. The audit will either confirm those numbers or correct them.

The headline number to watch

General Fund cash on hand at June 30, 2024. The FY24 CAR reports $25.91 million. The four-year audited trend points to roughly $19–20 million. The next year's internal management number is $19.3 million. The number in the FY24 CAR sits about $6 million above the trend line, with no operational story that explains the jump up or the snap back down.

That cash number is the easiest anomaly to see, but it's probably not the most serious one. The single largest issue on the FY24 state-filed report is a $19 million shortfall in the SAVE Fund — the capital projects fund that is also the largest single piece of the district's overall financial position. The General Fund cash spike, the interfund balance explosion, and the Management Fund growth are all part of the same picture, but the SAVE shortfall is the one with the biggest dollar magnitude and the most direct consequences for the district's existing bondholders. The K.02.01 memo from the district's own COO has already explained much of the mechanism behind these findings.

What "General Fund cash on hand" actually means

The General Fund is the district's main checking account — the bucket that pays teachers, runs buildings, buys textbooks, and keeps the lights on. "Cash on hand" is what's actually in the bank at the end of the fiscal year. It is the most basic measure of whether a district has the money to pay its bills.

Schools usually express this as "days of cash" — how many days the district could keep operating if no new money came in. Rating agencies and Iowa school finance experts generally consider 60 days a healthy cushion. Iowa City has been below that for years, but the trend was steady, predictable, and visible. The FY24 CAR breaks that pattern.

Underneath the cash spike: the SAVE Fund shortfall

The General Fund cash number is the easiest anomaly to see, but the FY24 CAR has three other findings that matter more. The biggest of them is in the SAVE Fund — the bucket that pays for the district's capital projects. The interfund balance explosion and the Management Fund property tax shift are part of the same picture, but the SAVE shortfall is where the largest dollar amount lives and where the most serious downstream consequences sit.

Before each finding, the plain-language version. The technical detail follows for anyone who wants it.

Finding 1 — The SAVE Fund spent $19 million more than it was allowed to

The SAVE Fund is the bucket that pays for capital projects like building expansions and renovations. It's funded by a statewide one-cent sales tax and by sales-tax revenue bonds. By law, it can only spend what it has on hand from those two sources.

Plain-language version

In FY24, capital project spending out of SAVE ran $19 million past what the bond and sales tax revenue could legitimately cover. Picture a household savings account dedicated to home renovations. By household rule, you can only spend what's in that account on the renovation. In FY24, the renovation spending ran $19,000 past what the renovation savings could cover — and the books show the shortfall.

This appears on the FY24 CAR balance sheet as a negative $18,944,278 in SAVE's unassigned fund balance. A negative number in that column is unusual; the fund went from $0 a year earlier to -$18.9M.

What caused the deficit isn't directly documented in public board materials. The K.02.01 Capital Outlay Borrowing Need Memo and the companion K.02.02 strategy document, both presented at the March 24, 2026 board meeting, describe a misallocation pattern occurring in October 2025 (FY26) and use the phrase "past practice" to refer to routing Debt Service property tax receipts into the Schoolhouse Fund instead of into Debt Service. The "past practice" language suggests the pattern existed across multiple prior fiscal years and may have been active in FY24 too — but that's an inference, not direct evidence. The memos document October 2025, not FY24.

And the simple misallocation mechanism the memos describe doesn't, by itself, produce a SAVE deficit. If Debt Service property tax receipts were being deposited into the Schoolhouse Fund (which includes SAVE), that would have *increased* SAVE's revenue side, not produced a $19 million shortfall. For SAVE to show a deficit, something else has to be happening — possibly overspending on FMP 2.0 capital projects running ahead of available bond proceeds, possibly revenue or receivable recognition timing, possibly a compliance issue around how restricted bond proceeds were used. The FY24 audit will have to surface the actual mechanism. The public memos don't.

Why this is the most serious of the four findings

The SAVE Fund isn't an obscure capital fund. ICCSD has roughly $123 million in outstanding SAVE revenue bonds — the 2022A and 2023 series — and those bonds are repaid from future SAVE receipts. The bond agreements include coverage ratio covenants requiring SAVE revenues to be a defined multiple of the annual debt service payments. A SAVE fund running a $19 million deficit raises a direct question about whether those covenants are being met.

That question doesn't go away with a CAR-to-audit reclassification. Even if the audit moves revenue or expenditure between funds to reduce the deficit on paper, the bondholders, the rating agencies, and the district's outside counsel will all be looking at this number specifically. It's the kind of finding that triggers required notices to bondholders under the district's continuing disclosure agreements, and it's the kind of number a rating agency notices when ICCSD eventually engages on rating recovery.

Finding 2 — The district's funds were lending each other unusual amounts of money

Iowa law requires school districts to keep money in separate buckets by purpose. Think of them as separate checking accounts in a single household, each with rules about what it can pay for:

  • General Fund — operations, payroll, supplies. The big one.
  • Management Fund — liability insurance, workers' comp, unemployment, early retirement.
  • Debt Service Fund — bond principal and interest payments.
  • SAVE Fund — capital projects (funded by sales tax + bonds).
  • PPEL Fund — facility maintenance and small construction.

When one bucket pays for something that belongs to another bucket, the district records an IOU between them. These IOUs are normally tiny and get settled quickly. In FY24, they exploded.

Plain-language version

About $11 million of the Management Fund's cash sitting in the bank effectively belongs to the General Fund. The General Fund's reported cash position depends on whether those IOUs get treated as good as cash. The audit will test that.

This reframes the original cash anomaly. The FY24 CAR is not just saying "the General Fund had more cash than the trend would predict." It is saying "the General Fund's reported cash position depends on a $14 million IOU from other buckets — mostly from the Management Fund — that didn't exist at this scale a year earlier." Whether the audit accepts those IOUs decides whether the reported cash is real.

Finding 3 — Most of the property tax increase went to one bucket

Property tax growth normally gets spread across several buckets — the General Fund (operations), the Debt Service Fund (bond payments), the PPEL Fund (facilities), and the Management Fund (insurance and retirement). The split depends on the levy rates the board sets at budget time.

Plain-language version

In FY24, the district's property tax revenue grew by $10.2 million. $8.3 million of that — about 82% — went to the Management Fund alone. Only $1.7 million went to the General Fund. That kind of one-bucket concentration is unusual.

Management Fund property tax revenue went from $5.2 million in FY23 to $13.5 million in FY24 (+161%). Management Fund cash followed it: $1.14 million to $13.09 million (+1,049%).

The board can decide to raise the Management Levy, and that would be legal. The audit's Required Supplementary Information will show the certified Management Levy rate. If the rate supports that much revenue, this was a deliberate board decision to build up the Management Fund. If not, the audit reclassifies the receipts back to the buckets they belong in. Either way, this is a question with a definite answer once the audit is published.

A quick note: cash vs. IOU

Before the summary, one piece of plumbing that matters. A school district's balance sheet records two different things that both contribute to a fund's reported strength:

  • Cash & investments — actual money sitting in the bank, attributed to that bucket.
  • Interfund receivable (the IOU) — a recorded claim that another bucket owes this bucket money.

Both appear as assets on the balance sheet. But they are not the same thing. Cash is cash. An IOU is a claim — useful only if the other bucket can actually pay. When you hear about the General Fund's $25.9M cash, that is the bank balance. When you hear about the $14M interfund receivable, that is a separate line on the balance sheet for IOUs other buckets owe to the General Fund. The audit's job is to decide whether the IOUs are legitimate.

A possible explanation — not yet confirmed

One reasonable hypothesis ties the four findings together. During FY24, some property tax money may have been routed into the wrong buckets, capital project work continued at the planned pace, and at year-end the books were balanced using IOUs between the buckets. The General Fund's reported strength would then come from both $25.9 million of actual bank cash and a $14 million IOU from other buckets — both counted as assets on the balance sheet.

The district has acknowledged a similar misallocation pattern in October 2025 (FY26) and described it as "past practice." That language suggests the pattern existed across multiple prior years and may have been active in FY24 too. But this is a hypothesis, not a confirmed explanation. The K.02.01 and K.02.02 memos document October 2025, not FY24. Whether the same mechanism caused the FY24 numbers is something only the FY24 audit can establish.

What might have happened in FY24, step by step

This is a hypothesis. Each step is labeled by whether the FY24 state-filed report directly shows it, or whether it's an inference from the district's later disclosures.

  1. Property tax money may have been routed into the wrong buckets. Inference, not observed in FY24 records. The district's later "past practice" language suggests this. The FY24 state-filed report does show Management Fund property tax growing 161% while General Fund tax grew only 2%, which is consistent with the pattern but not proof of it.
  2. Capital project work on FMP 2.0 continued at the planned pace. Documented. FMP 2.3 wasn't approved until spring 2025, so it was not yet a factor in FY24. SAVE Fund spending in FY24 was about FMP 2.0 — building expansions and renovations financed by the 2022A and 2023 SAVE revenue bonds.
  3. At year-end, the books show large IOUs between the buckets. Observed directly. The General Fund records itself as owed $14 million from other buckets. The Management Fund records itself as owing $11.3 million back. What caused the IOUs to exist at this scale — whether it's the misallocation pattern or something else — is a separate question the audit will answer.
  4. The General Fund's reported strength sits above the four-year trend. Observed directly. $25.9 million in actual bank cash, plus a $14 million IOU as a separate asset line, vs roughly $19 million on trend. Whether the audit accepts those numbers as legitimate determines whether the General Fund's reported position holds up.
  5. The SAVE Fund ended FY24 with a $19 million shortfall. Observed directly. Mechanism not yet known. The simple misallocation theory doesn't actually explain a deficit (it would inflate SAVE revenue, not produce a shortfall). The audit will have to identify what specifically caused capital spending to outpace available SAVE resources.

The audit's job is to test each of these. If the audit's restatement note and internal controls findings confirm the misallocation hypothesis, the story holds. If they show a different mechanism — or nothing at all — the story shifts to whatever the audit actually surfaces. The point of this page isn't to predict the answer. It's to know which cells to compare.

What this is not about

One thing worth setting aside: the $10 million interfund transfer from the Health Insurance Fund to the General Fund happened in August 2025. That transfer is part of the FY26 fiscal year story and will be addressed in the FY26 audit when that lands in March 2027. It has no effect on the FY24 numbers or the FY24 audit.

This page also does not allege wrongdoing. The FY24 CAR was filed by district staff in September 2024 using the district's own accounting records as they existed at that time. The FY24 audit's job is to verify those records independently. The most common explanation for a CAR-to-audit gap is a fund-classification adjustment caught during audit fieldwork — that is exactly what audits exist to do.

The watchlist

The audit is expected in July 2026. When it comes out, these are the things to compare against the version the district already filed with the state. The first three are where the answer would tell the public the most. The other five fill in the picture for anyone who wants to follow more closely.

If you only read three

  1. Whether the IOUs between buckets are real The biggest single question the audit will answer.
    The version the district already filed says the General Fund is owed $14.08 million from other buckets, and the Management Fund owes $11.31 million back.
    If the audit confirms those numbers, the original classification was accepted as legitimate.
    If the audit reduces them, both the General Fund's reported strength and the Management Fund's cash buildup shrink at the same time.
  2. The $19 million SAVE Fund shortfall The most serious single anomaly in the report the district filed.
    The state-filed report says SAVE spent $18.9 million more on capital projects than the bond and sales tax revenue could legitimately cover. A shortfall this size on a major capital fund is unusual.

    Why this matters most: ICCSD has roughly $123 million in outstanding SAVE revenue bonds, and those bonds are repaid from future SAVE receipts under specific coverage ratio covenants. A negative SAVE fund balance raises a direct question about whether those covenants are being met. The audit will either confirm the deficit (triggering required disclosure to bondholders and rating agency questions), reclassify revenue into SAVE (a clear fund-classification finding), or restate prior years (a multi-year reporting integrity finding). All three outcomes are problematic for the district's bond rating recovery plan.
  3. Whether the district stayed inside its legal budget The most consequential number for state oversight.
    The state-filed report has the district inside its legal spending limit by +1.67%, a cushion of about $3.3 million.
    If the audit catches bills that were owed but not yet recorded — federal payroll tax penalties, special education shortfall, construction commitments, retirement obligations — that cushion can disappear easily.
    If it flips negative, ICCSD exceeded its legal spending limit two years in a row. The dedicated page below walks through what that would mean.

More for the detail-oriented reader

  1. The General Fund's actual bank cash The number that most directly affects the days-of-cash story above.
    The state-filed report claims $25.9 million in the General Fund bank account at June 30, 2024.
    If the audit shows $19–21 million, the "money in the wrong buckets" theory is confirmed.
    If the audit shows $24 million or more, the year-after cash drop to $19.3 million needs its own explanation, which the district hasn't yet provided publicly.
  2. Whether the audit has to redo prior-year numbers Audits add a note when previously reported numbers were wrong.
    A note in the FY24 audit that says the prior year's General Fund, Management Fund, Debt Service Fund, or SAVE Fund balances were restated is direct confirmation that money had been put in the wrong bucket.
  3. The Management Fund's $13 million cash buildup Tests whether the 82% property tax shift was a board decision or a classification error.
    The state-filed report shows Management Fund cash at $13.09 million and property tax revenue at $13.48 million.
    The audit will show the Management Levy rate the board certified. If the rate supports that much revenue, it was a deliberate board decision. If not, the audit moves the receipts back to the buckets they belong in.
  4. Whether the Debt Service Fund's tax money was actually there The mirror image of the SAVE shortfall.
    The state-filed report shows Debt Service Fund cash at $2.16 million and property tax revenue at $12.95 million.
    If the audit moves $5–10 million of property tax receipts from the Management Fund or General Fund into Debt Service, both the SAVE shortfall and the General Fund cash overstatement get untangled at the same time.
  5. Whether the auditors flag the district's accounting controls The auditors' separate report on whether the district's accounting systems are catching errors.
    The FY23 audit flagged two "significant deficiencies" and one "material weakness." If the FY24 audit adds a finding about keeping money in the right buckets, that is the institutional-level confirmation that the problem isn't a one-time mistake.

Why this matters now

The district is rebuilding its forecast, working through an audit backlog, and trying to restore the credibility that supports a future bond rating. The FY24 audit is the first independent look at the district's books in two years. A clean audit that closely matches the CAR would be a real positive signal that the underlying systems were healthier than the post-crisis reporting suggests. An audit that materially restates FY24 would confirm what the board's own internal reporting has been pointing at since the K.02.01 memo in March 2026.

Either outcome is informative. The point of this page is to make sure the right comparisons get done in plain view when the audit lands — not after months of explanation by people with reason to soften it.
