ICCSD · FY24 Audit Summary

The FY24 audit, in plain terms

The Iowa City Community School District's independent audit for the year ended June 30, 2024 — the "Financial and Compliance Report" — was released with an auditor's report dated June 10, 2026. It is 124 pages. This page summarizes what it says: the opinions, the financial picture, the findings, and how the audited numbers compare to the unaudited report the district filed with the state in 2024. It is the first independent audit of the district's books in two years.

The two-minute version

The auditor issued an unmodified ("clean") opinion on the financial statements — the most important single result, and a positive one. It means the statements present the district's financial position fairly, in all material respects, under generally accepted accounting principles. The auditor expressed no doubt about the district's ability to continue operating.

That clean opinion sits alongside an unusually heavy set of findings: fourteen numbered findings, including five material weaknesses in financial reporting, two qualified opinions on federal programs, a prior-period restatement, and several violations of Iowa statutory requirements — among them exceeding the certified budget and moving money between funds without the board authorization the law requires. The district was not classified as a low-risk auditee.

Several of the figures that looked alarming in the unaudited report the district filed in 2024 were corrected or reclassified by the audit rather than confirmed — most notably an apparent $18.9 million deficit in the capital-projects (SAVE) fund, which the audit shows as a positive balance. The substance of the audit's concerns is in the district's internal controls, federal-grant compliance, budget compliance, and the timeliness and accuracy of its reporting, not in a single missing pile of cash.

What this document is

Every Iowa school district files an annual financial report with the state — the Certified Annual Report (CAR) — using its own numbers, roughly three months after the fiscal year ends. A separate independent audit examines the same year later, tests the numbers, and can correct them. This page summarizes the audit. Iowa City filed its FY24 CAR in September 2024; the FY24 audit's report is dated June 10, 2026, and was filed with the state in June 2026.

Two timing facts matter for reading it. First, this is FY24 — the year ended June 30, 2024. Events from later years (the August 2025 interfund loan from the health-insurance fund, the 2026 cash-flow borrowing) belong to FY25 and FY26 and are not part of these statements. Second, the audit was completed late: the audit itself reports that the district did not file its FY23 or FY24 single audits within the nine-month federal deadline.

The opinions

An audit is built around formal opinions. This report contains three layers:

  • Financial statements — Unmodified (clean). The statements present fairly, in all material respects, the district's financial position and results for the year.
  • Federal programs — mixed. The Child Nutrition Cluster received an unmodified opinion. The Special Education Cluster and the Education Stabilization Fund (the COVID-relief / ESSER programs) each received a qualified opinion — meaning the auditor found a specific compliance problem significant enough to except from an otherwise clean conclusion.
  • Auditee risk status — not low-risk. Because of the findings, the district does not qualify as a low-risk auditee, which generally means more extensive federal audit testing going forward.

The report also carries an Emphasis-of-Matter paragraph drawing attention to a prior-period restatement (below). An emphasis-of-matter paragraph does not change the opinion; it flags something the reader should notice.

On an expenditures basis, the General Fund's $22.5 million of cash is roughly 41 days of operating cash ($22.5M divided by daily expenditures). That is below the 60-day cushion rating agencies generally consider healthy, consistent with the district's multi-year trend, and lower than the $25.9 million the district had reported in its unaudited CAR.

On the SAVE revenue bonds, the audit reports incremental sales-tax revenue of $19.02 million against principal and interest paid of $14.15 million for the year — coverage of about 1.34×. The report discloses no covenant violation. This is the FY24 position; the strain on SAVE cash flow that surfaced in later years is a FY26 matter outside this report.

The findings

The audit lists fourteen numbered findings plus a set of statutory-reporting items. They fall into four groups. The severity tags follow the audit's own categories: a material weakness is the most serious internal-control category; a significant deficiency is less severe but still merits governance attention; noncompliance means a legal or grant requirement was not met.

On 2024-008: the audit's interfund note shows $38.2 million of balances owed between funds at year-end (the General Fund alone is owed $29.1 million and owes $20.1 million). Iowa law requires the board to approve interfund loans in advance, set repayment by October 1 of the following year, and charge interest. The audit states those requirements were not met.

Statutory reporting

  • IV-A-24 — Certified budget exceeded. Expenditures exceeded the certified budget in the support function (before the May 2024 amendment) and in the other function (after amendment). On the audit's budget schedule, the "other" function ran about $19.5 million over its amended budget, and total expenditures of $341.9 million exceeded the amended budget of $329.5 million.
  • IV-H-24 / IV-I-24 — variances in certified enrollment and supplementary weighting reported to the state in October 2023.
  • IV-J-24 — the district lacked a formal depository resolution.
  • IV-N-24 — the 509A certificate of compliance was not filed timely.

Repeat findings

The audit's summary of prior-year findings shows that several FY23 issues were marked "not corrected," with the district attributing the recurrence to significant turnover in the business office. The FY23 material weakness on significantly delayed and revised financials, the bank-reconciliation deficiency, and the late single-audit filing each carried forward into FY24.

The headline change is the SAVE fund: the CAR reported an apparent $18.9 million deficit, and the audit shows a positive $14.7 million balance, indicating the CAR figure was a reporting/classification error rather than a real shortfall. General Fund cash came down by about $3.4 million. The Management Levy Fund's reported cash and property-tax revenue were left essentially as filed. Reading the two documents together, the audit's corrections were in the district's favor on the most alarming number and against it on budget compliance.

What the audit does and does not establish

It establishes: a clean opinion on the financial statements; no going-concern doubt; correction of the apparent SAVE deficit; positive (if modest) SAVE bond coverage for the year; and a defined, finite list of control and compliance problems with a management response attached to each.

It also establishes: five material weaknesses in financial reporting, two qualified federal-program opinions including unallowable COVID-grant costs, a $6.15 million prior-period restatement, interfund borrowing conducted without the required board authorization, a certified budget exceeded in two functions, late single-audit filings, and several uncorrected repeat findings — together with non-low-risk auditee status.

It does not establish intent or wrongdoing; the auditor does not make that judgment. It does not cover FY25 or FY26 events. And a clean opinion on the statements is not a statement that the district's systems are healthy — the separate findings report is where that question is answered, and there the answer is that the control environment had material weaknesses during FY24.

The bottom line

The numbers, once audited, are less dramatic than the unaudited filing suggested — the SAVE "deficit" was an error, and there is no missing cash. The audit's substance is that the district's internal controls, federal-grant compliance, budget compliance, and reporting timeliness fell materially short in FY24, with several problems repeating from the prior year. The district has issued a corrective action plan responding to every finding.
