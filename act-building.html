Iowa City Community School District · a closer look

The headquarters they bought, and the one they sold

An $8.7M building bought going into the squeeze, and a $3.2M sale coming out of it.

A companion to "How Iowa City ran out of room," built from contemporaneous reporting.

In 2022, with the building program already underway, the district bought itself a bigger headquarters. It paid about $8.7M for ACT's Tyler Building and moved its central offices in. Four years later, short of cash, it sold its old headquarters for $3.2M. The two transactions, read together, are a small study in how the district spent.

What it bought, and for how much

The purchase was an $8.7M administrative building, paid from the physical-plant levy. The Tyler Building, about 85,000 square feet on roughly eight acres, became the district's Center for Innovation and the home of its central administration.1

The price sat well above the building's assessed value. The county assessed it at about $5.4M. The board approved the purchase, in the words of contemporaneous coverage, "not without questions."2 The physical-plant levy that paid for it is restricted to buildings and equipment, so the money was legally available for this even as the operating budget tightened. That is the point worth sitting with: the rules let the district buy an $8.7M headquarters in the same window its General Fund cushion was thinning.

The timing

The purchase came during the most expensive stretch in the district's history. The 2017 bond was being spent down, capital balances were already committed, and the operating reserves had been sliding since 2018. Buying a larger administrative home in that window was a choice about priorities, made with restricted dollars that could not have closed the operating gap, but that still signaled where attention was going.

The reversal

By 2026 the district was selling property to raise cash. It sold its former headquarters at 1725 North Dodge Street to the City of Iowa City for $3.2M.3 The sequence is hard to miss: $8.7M for a bigger headquarters going into the squeeze, $3.2M for the old one coming out of it.

What this shows, and what it does not

The purchase was legal and the levy was the right one to use. This is not a finding of wrongdoing, and nothing here turns on who sold the building. It is a capital-allocation decision, made in public, that residents questioned at the time and that reads differently next to what came after.13 The broader story is the same one the rest of the project tells: the district had room to spend in some places and no room at all in others, and the walls between those pots of money meant a handsome headquarters and a payroll scramble could be true at the same time.
