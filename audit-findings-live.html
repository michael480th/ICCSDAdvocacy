What is a “federal finding”?

The federal audit, in plain English

Any district that spends enough federal money in a year (Title I, special education/IDEA, school nutrition, the COVID-era ESSER funds) gets a second, separate audit called a Single Audit, run under federal rules. It asks a different question than the regular audit: not “are the books right?” but “did the district follow the rules attached to the federal dollars?”

The auditor issues an opinion on compliance for each major program. “Unmodified” is clean. A “qualified” opinion is the auditor formally stating the district did not comply in some material way — the federal equivalent of a material weakness. It can come with “questioned costs,” dollars flagged as possibly spent improperly.

Each district's worst federal result, FY2020–2025. Most sail through clean every year. A handful drew a minor federal finding but kept a clean opinion. Only two ever drew a qualified opinion — and Iowa City is one. Bars show federal material weaknesses in the district's worst year; Iowa City in red.

Case study: how Davenport climbed out

Davenport is the one large district that has been somewhere like where Iowa City is now — and worked its way back. It had material weaknesses three years running and a qualified federal opinion, then drove its findings to zero. The weaknesses were strikingly similar to Iowa City's.

What the weaknesses were — and how they fixed them: year-end close (material corrections in capital assets, accrued liabilities and AP their controls missed → built reconciliation and review procedures); federal reporting (their Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards was off by $1.84 million → reconcile it to the ledger with a Finance review before certifying); and payroll charged to federal funds (no supervisory time approval → require it and integrate the time-clock). Nearly the same failure points as Iowa City's FY2024 report.

The honest caveat. Davenport is a fair role model, not a perfect mirror. Even its worst year (FY2020: three material weaknesses) was milder than Iowa City's FY2024 (eight, five of them financial). Iowa City is starting from a deeper hole — so Davenport's roughly four-year climb is a floor on the effort, not a ceiling.
