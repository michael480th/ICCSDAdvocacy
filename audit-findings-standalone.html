Iowa's 15 largest districts · FY2020–FY2025

How many audit findings is normal?

Iowa City's recent audits drew an unusual number of findings — including several “material weaknesses,” an auditor's most serious red flag about how a district controls its money. This puts that in context: every large Iowa district, side by side, so you can see what's typical and where Iowa City actually stands.

The short version
  • ⚠️A material weakness by itself is not unique. 4 of the 15 large districts had at least one material-weakness year in FY2020–2025 (Davenport CSD, Des Moines CSD, Iowa City CSD, Waterloo CSD). So “had a material weakness” alone would not make Iowa City an outlier.
  • 📉The volume and severity are what stand out. Iowa City's FY2024 audit carried 14 numbered findings, five of them financial-statement material weaknesses — the heaviest single-year load of any district in any year here. The next-worst peak at any peer was 8 findings.
  • 🚩And nothing else combines all of it. Most findings + five material weaknesses in one year + qualified federal opinions + repeat uncorrected items + an audit filed about two years late (which cost the district its bond rating). No peer district shows that combination.

Material weaknesses by district — worst single year

What's a “material weakness”?

It's an auditor's most serious warning short of finding an actual wrong number. It means the checks a district relies on to catch a big mistake — or theft — in its own books are broken badly enough that one could slip through unnoticed. The books may still be right; the safeguards around them are not.

Do these get fixed? Severity, not repetition

A natural follow-up: don't districts just fix these the next year? Mostly — no, and that turns out not to be what separates Iowa City.

What the prior-findings schedules show
  • ↻Repeat findings are common, not rare. Across the large districts, roughly 51% of one year's findings are still “Not corrected” a year later — most are low-level items that recur: segregation-of-duties deficiencies in small business offices, and statutory budget/enrollment variances. On the bare repeat rate, Iowa City is not unusual.
  • ⚠️Severity is the difference. Those recurring peer findings are almost never material weaknesses — only 4 districts ever had one at all. Iowa City's uncorrected items are material weaknesses: of its 7 prior findings, 6 were still “Not corrected” in FY2024, including the financial-reporting material weakness carried straight over from FY2023.
  • ✅Compare Davenport — the one real precedent. Davenport had material weaknesses three years running (FY2020–2022) and fixed them: down to a significant deficiency by FY2023 and a clean schedule (zero findings) by FY2025. Iowa City went the other way — one material weakness in FY2023 became five in FY2024.

So the honest distinction isn't that Iowa City fails to fix findings faster than its peers, or even that it has the most repeats (Johnston carried more low-level items forward). It is the only district where the most serious class of finding is both multiplying and going uncorrected.

What actually makes Iowa City the outlier

Not any single finding, but four things together — each compared with the peer group:

Reading this honestly. If the question is “is Iowa City the only large district that ever had a material weakness?” the answer is no — 4 of 15 did. The defensible claim is narrower and stronger: Iowa City's FY2024 is the most severe single-year audit result in the large-district group, and the surrounding pattern (late filing, lost rating, repeat findings, federal qualifications) is unmatched by any peer.
Bottom line

Yes — on the evidence, Iowa City is an outlier among Iowa's large districts, but in magnitude and pattern, not in merely having a finding. Five material weaknesses and 14 findings in one year, on top of a two-year-late audit and a withdrawn rating, is the worst combination in the group. The most credible way to say so is to show the distribution — which is this page.
