Iowa's 15 largest districts · FY2020–FY2025

How many audit findings is normal?

Iowa City's recent audits drew an unusual number of findings — including several “material weaknesses,” an auditor's most serious red flag about how a district controls its money. This puts that in context: every large Iowa district, side by side, so you can see what's typical and where Iowa City actually stands.

The short version
  • ⚠️A material weakness is serious — it's just not unique to Iowa City. Think of it like failing a class: not rare enough to be shocking, but never a good sign. In 91% of the district-years here (81 of 89) the auditor found none, and only 4 of the 15 large districts drew even one material-weakness year in FY2020–2025 (Davenport CSD, Des Moines CSD, Iowa City CSD, Waterloo CSD). Iowa City is one of them — so a material weakness by itself wouldn't make it an outlier. What follows is why it is.
  • 📉The severity is what stands out. Iowa City's FY2024 audit carried five financial-statement material weaknesses at once — the heaviest single-year load of any district in any year here. The most any peer ever showed in one year was two.
  • 🏠On the federal side, it is nearly alone. When an auditor reviews how federal money was spent, it issues an opinion on compliance. A qualified opinion — the auditor saying the district did not follow the rules in all material respects — is rare: across 15 districts and six years, only 2 ever received one (Davenport CSD, Iowa City CSD). Iowa City's FY2024 was qualified on two program clusters at once, with three federal material weaknesses on top.
  • 🚩And nothing else combines all of it. Most findings + five material weaknesses in one year + qualified federal opinions + repeat uncorrected items + an audit filed about two years late (which cost the district its bond rating). No peer district shows that combination.

Material weaknesses by district — worst single year

What's a “material weakness”?

It's an auditor's most serious warning short of finding an actual wrong number. It means the checks a district relies on to catch a big mistake — or theft — in its own books are broken badly enough that one could slip through unnoticed. The books may still be right; the safeguards around them are not.

What is a “federal finding”?

The federal audit, in plain English

Any district that spends enough federal money in a year (think Title I, special education/IDEA, school nutrition, and the COVID-era ESSER funds) gets a second, separate audit called a Single Audit, run under federal rules (the Uniform Guidance). It asks a different question than the regular audit: not “are the books right?” but “did the district follow the rules that come attached to the federal dollars?”

The auditor then issues an opinion on compliance for each major federal program. “Unmodified” is the clean result. A “qualified” opinion is the auditor formally stating the district did not comply, in some material way, with the federal requirements — and it can come with “questioned costs,” dollars the auditor flags as possibly spent improperly. A qualified opinion is the federal equivalent of the material-weakness warning above.

The significant issues, in plain English

The two charts above show where Iowa City stands out. This is what those findings actually were — translated out of audit language. All are from the district's FY2024 audit (findings 2024-001 through 2024-014).

What the auditor actually found
  • 🏦The bank accounts weren't being reconciled. Reconciling a bank account is critical because it is the one routine check that proves the district's own records match the money the bank actually holds — it's how missing, duplicated, or stolen funds get caught early. For FY2024 those reconciliations weren't done on time, so that check simply wasn't happening. In the auditor's words, errors or “misappropriations of assets” could occur and go undetected. (A repeat finding; the stated cause was turnover in key accounting staff.)
  • 📊The year-end statements were materially wrong as first prepared. Receivables, payables, and capital assets weren't properly adjusted, so the financial statements were misstated and needed material corrections caught during the audit — not by the district's own process. (Also a repeat finding.)
  • 👤One person could run an entire money cycle. The district lacked segregation of duties over payroll, cash disbursements, and school receipts — the basic separation that keeps a single error or theft from slipping through unnoticed.
  • 🏢Federal COVID money went to unallowable costs — and records were missing. The district charged the ESSER program for costs that weren't allowed, and for one program couldn't produce the expenditure records at all. These are the findings behind the qualified federal opinion above.
  • ⏳The audit was about two years late — and it cost the bond rating. The district didn't complete its FY2023 and FY2024 single audits within the required nine months. The delay left lenders and the public without current numbers, and the district's bond rating was withdrawn.
  • 💸Smaller, but telling. A student activity fund ran a deficit, and money was moved between funds without the formal authorization the rules require.

Case study: how Davenport climbed out

Davenport is the one large district that has been somewhere like where Iowa City is now — and worked its way back. It had material weaknesses three years running and a qualified federal opinion, then drove its findings to zero. The arc is worth studying because the weaknesses were strikingly similar to Iowa City's.

What the weaknesses were — and how they fixed them
  • 🔍Year-end close (2020-001). The audit turned up material corrections in capital assets, accrued liabilities, and accounts payable that Davenport's own controls would never have caught — “inadequate reconciliation and internal review.” Fix: they built reconciliation and review procedures over the balance-sheet accounts.
  • 🏛️Federal reporting (2020-002). Their Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards was misstated by $1.84 million — it didn't reconcile to the books, mostly mis-coded ESSER money. Fix: reconcile the schedule to the general ledger with a separate Finance review before anything is certified to the state.
  • 💰Payroll charged to federal funds (2020-005, the federal material weakness). Timesheets weren't being approved before payroll ran, and the time-clock didn't talk to the general ledger. Fix: require supervisory approval of time and move toward integrated, batch-processed payroll.

Those are nearly the same failure points in Iowa City's FY2024 report: year-end close, federal-award reporting and allowable costs, and payroll controls. Davenport shows the climb is doable — findings dropped and downgraded every single year (material weakness → significant deficiency → resolved) on the back of assigned ownership and review-before-certify discipline, not a single dramatic move.

The honest caveat. Davenport is a fair role model, not a perfect mirror. Even its worst year (FY2020: three material weaknesses) was milder than Iowa City's FY2024 (eight, five of them financial). Iowa City is starting from a deeper hole — so Davenport's roughly four-year climb is a floor on the effort, not a ceiling.

Reading this honestly. If the question is “is Iowa City the only large district that ever had a material weakness?” the answer is no — 4 of 15 did. The defensible claim is narrower and stronger: Iowa City's FY2024 is the most severe single-year audit result in the large-district group, and the surrounding pattern (late filing, lost rating, repeat findings, federal qualifications) is unmatched by any peer.
Bottom line

Yes — on the evidence, Iowa City is an outlier among Iowa's large districts, but in magnitude and pattern, not in merely having a finding. Five material weaknesses and 14 findings in one year, on top of a two-year-late audit and a withdrawn rating, is the worst combination in the group. The most credible way to say so is to show the distribution — which is this page.
