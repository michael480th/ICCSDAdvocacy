Iowa City Community School District

How Iowa City ran out of room

A district built the biggest school project in state history, then went looking for cash to make payroll. The two are connected, and they are not the same problem.

A data-driven account, built from audited reports, state filings, and contemporaneous coverage.

About the figures. Numbers through fiscal year 2024 are from audited reports. Fiscal year 2025 is from the district's own unaudited filings, and 2026 figures are projections or contemporaneous reporting. Each is labeled where it appears, and every claim is sourced in the notes at the end.

In January 2026, Iowa City's school board learned it had borrowed $10M that no board member had approved. The money had come out of the district's health insurance fund the prior August, to cover payroll.1 It was left out of the quarterly report the board actually saw. The board got the full story months later.

Six years earlier, the same district passed the largest school bond in Iowa history. Voters approved $191.5M in 2017 to rebuild and expand its schools.2 By its own numbers at the time, the district was in good shape.

The gap between those two facts is the story. Two separate failures arrived at the same time, and they drained the same bank account. One was a decade of building. The other was an operating budget that slowly stopped balancing. Neither caused the other. They met in the district's checkbook, at the worst possible moment.

Read the chart as the whole story in one line. That is the district's cash, counted in days of spending. It ran close to three months of cash in the mid 2010s. The audited line ends in 2024. The dotted tail is the district's own unaudited figure for 2025 and a 2026 projection of about a week of cash, before any emergency borrowing.3

It did not start in trouble

In 2016 the district looked healthy on every measure that matters. The General Fund held about $33M in cash, close to 84 days of spending.4 Its solvency ratio, reserves against a year of revenue, sat above 11%, inside the range Iowa treats as healthy. Its spending authority, the number the state watches most closely, still had room.

The district even graded its own books. In 2019 its chief financial officer wrote a 22-page financial health report, scoring ten financial ratios green, yellow, or red against targets.5 That is a finance office that knew exactly what to watch, and put it in writing for the board to read.

That same report sounded the first alarm. It noted solvency had slipped from 11% to 7%, below the 10% level it said bond rating agencies look for. It noted days of cash had fallen to 63, short of the 90-day target. The warning was on the page in 2019, in the district's own hand.

So the capacity to see the problem existed. The rest of this story is about what happened to the people, the committee, and the habits that produced that 2019 report. Within a few years, all three were gone.

The biggest bet in the state

In September 2017 voters approved $191.5M in bonds, the largest school bond in Iowa history.2 It paid for the Facilities Master Plan, a decade of construction across the district. The district issued the bonds in three pieces between 2017 and 2020.

One feature of Iowa school finance explains most of what follows. Bond money is walled off. It goes into a separate construction fund, restricted to the approved projects, and it is paid back by its own property tax levy. It cannot pay a teacher, and the operating budget cannot touch it. So the building program and the day-to-day budget were two different stories from the very start. A district can build a great deal and still run its operations into the ground, because the two run on separate tracks.

The district did not stop at the bond. It kept stacking capital debt, mostly through sales-tax (SAVE) revenue bonds, including a $66M issue in 2022 and a $71M issue in 2023. Total debt peaked near $321M in 2023.6 That left Iowa City with one of the largest sales-tax debt loads of any large district in the state, most of its penny-sales-tax revenue pledged to bond payments for years out.

It also spent capital cash closer to home. In 2022 the district paid $8.7M for ACT's Tyler Building, using its physical-plant levy, and moved its central offices there.7 The price came in well above the building's $5.4M assessed value, and the purchase drew public objection at the time. Some of the residents who questioned it would spend the next three years warning the board, in writing, about nearly everything else that went wrong.

A short guide to how the money works

Iowa runs its schools on two separate meters, and most people only watch one. The first is cash, the money in the bank. The second is spending authority, a state-set ceiling on how much a district may spend at all, no matter how much cash it holds.8 A district can sit on cash and still run out of authority, or keep authority while the cash drains. Iowa City managed to do both at once.

The funds are walled off from each other by law. Operating money lives in the General Fund. Bond proceeds live in a construction fund. Sales-tax money, the physical-plant levy, and debt service each have their own fund. Money raised for one purpose generally cannot be spent on another. That is why building cannot fund teaching, and teaching cannot raid the building money.

One detail breaks the tidy picture. All those separate funds share a single bank account. The district pools its cash and tracks each fund's claim on the pool as a bookkeeping entry.6 So a fund can spend more cash than it holds and quietly borrow from the others in the pool. Hold onto that. It is how two unrelated problems ended up in the same hole.

A fair question: aren't the funds separate? They are, by law, and that separation is real. It does not mean the building money and the operating money never met. Two things connect them. First, the separate funds share one bank account, so spending in one draws down the cash available to all of them, ledgers or no ledgers. Second, the funds were in fact mixed: the FY2024 audit found $38M of loans moving between funds without the board votes Iowa law requires, and the following year $10M moved from the insurance fund to cover payroll the same way.6 The rule keeps the funds apart on paper. The cash sat in one account.

The operating budget stops balancing

While the district built, its day-to-day budget slowly came apart. The cause was not exotic. Salaries and benefits ran about 85% of the General Fund, year after year, while enrollment flattened and state aid stayed thin.4 Costs rose faster than the money allowed to cover them.

Every operating cushion drained on the same schedule, and each fell below the line the district had drawn for itself. The district's own 2019 financial health report set the target for days of cash at 90, and treated solvency under 10% as a warning, the level it noted that bond rating agencies watch.5 By 2022, spending authority had fallen from 6.6% of the limit to 0.1%, solvency from 12% to under 3%, and days of cash from 88 to 39.8 Each number was not just lower year over year. It was below the floor the district's own finance office had named. None of this had anything to do with construction. It was the operating model not adding up.

This is the half of the story that gets missed. The building program was loud and visible. The operating decline was quiet and on a different page. Because the two run on separate tracks, almost no one connected them until both hit bottom.

The alarms, and the committee built to answer them

The warnings were not subtle, and they came early. The district's own 2019 health report already flagged solvency below the rating-agency line and cash below target.5 By 2023, spending authority had gone negative, the unlawful level that puts a district under state review.

The district responded the right way, on paper. In November 2023, the same month it filed a corrective-action plan with the state, the board formed a Financial Oversight Committee to watch the finances.910 It met into early 2024.

Then it stopped. The committee went dark for about two years. When it finally reconvened in January 2026, as the crisis broke, a director opened the meeting by noting the obvious, that the group had not met in a couple of years.11 The two years it did not meet were the two years the rest of this story happened.

The audits stopped landing, too. The FY2023 audit, due in early 2024, did not arrive until August 2025, twenty-six months late, with a declared material weakness.6 In October 2024, Moody's withdrew the district's bond rating over the missing audits.12

Empty at the same time

By 2025 there was no slack left anywhere. The operating reserves were spent. The bond proceeds were spent. The sales-tax fund, the district's big capital reservoir, drained from $45.8M to $9.2M in a single year.13 Total pooled cash fell about 42% in 2025, from roughly $108M to $62M.

Look at how many funds emptied at once. Debt service ran down to about $200K. The student-activity fund went to zero. Two capital accounts went to zero. When every fund in the shared pool bottoms out together, a normal summer cash dip has nowhere to turn.

Borrowing from itself

With the pool dry, the funds began borrowing cash from each other to pay the bills. The FY2024 audit put a number on it. The district carried $38.2M of interfund loans at mid-2024, between funds, to cover what the auditors called cash-flowing expenditures.6

The auditors flagged it as breaking the law, not just the budget. Iowa rules require a formal board vote before one fund lends to another, full repayment by October 1 of the next year, and interest on the loan. The district did none of those.6 The board had not approved the loans. The deadline passed. No interest was paid.

One fund went underwater and could not be rescued. The student-activity fund overspent by about $1M and ended 2024 nearly $1M in the red, owing the other funds $1.5M.6 State law bars topping it up with a transfer, so the hole just sat there.

Who was watching

The CFO who wrote the district's 2019 financial health report retired in June 2023.5 His successor, the state later testified, lacked the school-business-official credential and was not really the district's financial leader.14

Bank reconciliations were not done for about three years. One finance officer could set up vendors, approve invoices, record the entries, and release the cash, with no second set of eyes.6 A staffer who joined in 2024 warned leadership of fundamental errors that October. The recorded reply was a two-word thank-you.15

The superintendent's contract carried no performance measures and was not tied to the district's goals. The board renewed it in July 2025, weeks before the crisis surfaced.16 When the failures came to light, the board moved the superintendent to a $180K role rather than removing him.17

Iowa City was watching

Iowa City is not a place that misses this kind of thing. It is a university town, thick with accountants, lawyers, auditors, and finance professionals, and the school board hears from that community constantly. On the money, people were paying close attention the whole way down.

They were watching when the district paid $8.7M for the ACT building, well above its $5.4M assessed value.7 They were watching when the audits stopped arriving on time, and when items left the board agenda without explanation right before votes to authorize tens of millions in new spending. The questions residents raised, in public and in writing, named the board's fiduciary duty directly.18

A year later, the State Board of Education would use the same word, fiduciary, to describe what had been missing.19 The community had said it first.

The reckoning

The bill came due in 2026, all at once. The hidden $10M loan surfaced in January.1 Payroll had run about $13.5M over budget, up 9% against a planned 2 to 3%, much of it a roughly $19M special-education shortfall.2021

Every lever the district reached for was already compromised. It approved $7.5M in cuts.22 It tried to borrow $25M, and every bank said no, because there were no current audits to underwrite it.23 It paused the $104M facilities plan and sold its old headquarters to the city for $3.2M to raise cash.24

The people at the top left. The superintendent, the deputy superintendent, and the CFO all departed. The bond rating was gone until at least 2028.17 The state opened oversight proceedings, with a takeover on the table.19 PFM projected the district would reach about a week of operating cash by mid-2026, not enough to cover July payroll without an emergency warrant.3

Why it happened

Strip away the noise and it is one mechanism. A debt-financed building program drained the shared cash pool from the capital side. A structural operating deficit drained the General Fund's claim from the other side. They were never the same problem, but they ran through one bank account, and they emptied it together. With no slack left, the funds cannibalized each other, and then the district reached into its insurance fund to make payroll.

The district paid about $525,000 in federal penalties for filing its payroll and excise taxes late. A federal tax lien was filed against it and then lifted; the state found the late filings kept recurring because no one was monitoring them.25 The district lost its bond rating and its bank credit, which left only costlier emergency borrowing. It owed interest on $38M of loans between its own funds and, by the auditors' finding, never paid it.6 In a single year its payroll ran about $13.5M over budget.20

The clearest proof is the other fourteen districts. This project benchmarks Iowa City against the fourteen other large districts in the state. They live under the same funding formula, the same enrollment trends, and the same school-choice rules. On the state's central health measure, almost all of them sit in comfortable territory. Iowa City sits alone at the bottom.

Fourteen districts lived under the same conditions and did not end up here. A district can do well in Iowa today, and most do. The difference was not the funding formula or the state's rules. It was everything that sat inside the district's own control. Whether that was error or something more is the question the board's own forensic audit is meant to answer.17

What to watch next

The full picture is still arriving. The FY2024 audit landed in June 2026, two years late. The FY2025 audit is expected around November 2026, and FY2026 around March 2027.17 Until then, the most recent years rest on unaudited figures.

Three things will tell you whether this is turning. Whether the rebuilt Financial Oversight Committee actually meets and bites. Whether the new CFO gets the audits and the monthly reporting current. And whether the forensic audit finds error or intent.19

One last number for context. A district that graded its own books in green, yellow, and red in 2019 could not produce a balance sheet by 2025. Everything in between is in the sources below.
