# Brief: The Facilities Master Plan dissent vs. this benchmark

**What this is.** A short reconciliation of the financial findings in this project's benchmark
([`iowa-district-financial-benchmark.md`](iowa-district-financial-benchmark.md) /
[`iccsd-vs-peers.html`](iccsd-vs-peers.html)) against the reasoning of an ICCSD Board member
(Mitch) who voted **"no"** on the Facilities Master Plan (FMP). His full statement is reproduced at
the bottom.

**Bottom line:** the data and the dissent agree on the financial substance. The board member then
raises three things the benchmark *cannot* see (and shouldn't pretend to), and the benchmark adds one
thing his statement underweights.

---

## Where the benchmark AGREES with the dissent

- **SAVE is over-leveraged / future flexibility is shrinking.** The benchmark finds ICCSD has pledged
  **~8.4 years of SAVE revenue — the 2nd-most of any large Iowa district** (vs. a ~5.4-year peer
  average), with current bonds amortizing to ~2036–37. That *is* the "historically 10–15 years"
  practice he describes; the proposal extending to **2047 would roughly double** it. → KPIs: *Years of
  SAVE money committed*, *Total building debt per student* ($22.4K, ~1.8× peers; one of the few
  districts stacking **GO + SAVE**), *Total forward debt (~$417M, highest in the set)*.
- **Flat enrollment vs. continued building.** The benchmark shows ICCSD enrollment is essentially
  **flat (+0.1%/yr)** — so its troubles are *not* caused by shrinking enrollment. That directly
  supports his "Why the rush to add capacity?" question. → KPI: *Enrollment trend*.
- **The district is poorly positioned to absorb new risk.** ICCSD scores **last of the 15** districts:
  spending authority went **negative in 2023**, reserves are ~2.5%, and **FY2024–FY2025 audits are
  unfiled**. Committing to a 22-year plan is riskier for a district that can't currently close its
  books on time. → KPIs: *Spending authority (UAB)*, *Reserves*, *Audit recency*, *Management quality*.

## Where the dissent goes BEYOND the benchmark (it adds genuine dimensions)

- **The 2047 horizon.** The benchmark is backward-looking (audited through FY2023). It can confirm the
  *current* leverage and trajectory, **not** independently verify the proposed 2047 commitment.
- **State-policy risk to SAVE.** The $900M state deficit and looming sales-/property-tax reform that
  could weaken SAVE/PPEL are macro risks the benchmark explicitly does **not** model.
- **Building capacity vs. enrollment.** He cites program capacity for 21,000+ and total capacity for
  28,000 against ~14,400 students. The benchmark measures capital *dollars* and enrollment *trend* —
  **not seat capacity / utilization**.
- **The political "ratchet"** (one high-school project commits the district to all three) and
  **investment-type evidence** (Biasi: infrastructure/HVAC/STEM > athletics for outcomes) are
  governance and program judgments outside a financial dataset.

## Nuances (not disagreements)

- The benchmark treats "building" as a neutral label, **not** inherently bad — but its
  **Capital-Sustainability score already penalizes building beyond means**, and ICCSD scores poorly
  there. So the method agrees the building is hard to sustain.
- **It's SAVE that's maxed, not all borrowing capacity.** ICCSD's GO debt is only ~21% of its 5% legal
  limit, so more GO borrowing is theoretically possible (with a voter referendum). This supports
  "slow down and preserve options" rather than contradicting it.

---

## Does the benchmark need refining? Are we missing KPIs?

**No errors to correct** — the dissent corroborates the financial findings; no re-weighting is needed.
But it surfaces useful gaps:

1. **Missing KPI — Capacity utilization (enrollment ÷ building capacity).** This is the single most
   relevant gap given the over-building concern, and the one KPI that would most directly test "Why the
   rush?" *It requires facility design-capacity data, which is not in audited financials or the state
   files used here* — it would need to be sourced (district facilities reports / FMP documents). Until
   then, "enrollment trend" is the closest available proxy.
2. **Enhancement — Forward-commitment horizon (final debt-payoff year).** A companion to the
   "years of SAVE committed" ratio that states the actual payoff year (≈2036–37 today; 2047 as
   proposed). Maps directly to the board member's framing; partially derivable from debt-maturity
   schedules already extracted.
3. **Documented limitation — the benchmark is backward-looking and district-level.** It cannot evaluate
   a *proposed* plan's future commitments or *state-policy* risk to SAVE revenue. This should be stated
   plainly so the analysis isn't over-read as forecasting.

**Recommended next step (optional):** add the *forward-commitment horizon* KPI (feasible from current
data), and note the *capacity-utilization* KPI as a planned addition pending facilities-capacity data.

## ICCSD Board member statement (Mitch), reproduced in full

> I appreciate the planning, collaboration, and thoughtfulness that have gone into the development of
> the Facilities Master Plan. I recognize that reasonable people may reach different conclusions based
> on the information and perspectives available to them. I will remain a 'no' vote on this plan, and
> I'd like to share my reasoning. I will reiterate, as I did at the last meeting, had I known the plan
> would leverage SAVE funding to 2047 back last August, I would have raised my red flag then. But, I
> learned of this a month ago.
>
> Before joining the Board, I was largely unaware of the weight of facilities decisions. I quickly
> learned of the weight when in the first four months, I was a part of the decision to close Hills
> Elementary. Within the first nine months, we were discussing a significant restructuring of our
> elementary schools. Having these kinds of experiences has made me cautious about long-term
> facilities planning.
>
> In 2013, the District projected enrollment of approximately 15,372 students by 2023. In 2015, the
> District projected enrollment at 16,317 students by 2025, but we have since built program capacity
> for over 21,000 students and total capacity for more than 28,000, even after closing a school. With
> the exception of Penn, elementary schools north of I-80 have had significant enrollment declines of
> 15 to 30%. Despite enrollment projections from both 2020 and late 2022/early 2023 indicating
> stagnation or decline, we have continued to build additional capacity. Again, these are the kinds of
> experiences that give me pause enough to say, "Why the rush?"
>
> In the background is a broader concern about the wider financial landscape we face as a state and as
> a nation. The original Facilities Master Plan and the 2020-update were developed under a very
> different state funding environment. This was a time when the state provided backfilled commercial
> property tax revenue, maintained a more balanced and less regressive tax structure, and enjoyed
> broader bipartisan support for public education. Iowa faces a $900M budget deficit, while there is a
> real potential for state-level tax reforms to hurt SAVE as a reliable source of revenue. Property
> taxes will be reformed next session and we will have less PPEL revenue. After property taxes, we know
> sales tax reform is next. Since our last meeting, I've had additional conversations with people who
> have a much deeper understanding of the state's fiscal outlook and the potential scope of upcoming
> tax reforms. The conversations only reinforced my concerns.
>
> Historically, the district has bonded SAVE revenues 10 to 15 years into the future. Committing SAVE
> funds to 2047, what is being proposed now, is a different level of risk. I look at the list of
> projects in front of me and I have to ask, "How confident am I that these are all the needs we will
> face over the next 22 or 23 years?" I have to be concerned with not just the projects in front of me
> today, but concern for the flexibility of future boards, administrators, and most importantly, our
> future students and their families.
>
> I have a firm understanding that politically, the moment that the ground is broken for a $5M or $12M
> project at one of our high schools is the moment we have committed to do it at the other two high
> schools, regardless of the cost. Once the ground is broken, our flexibility becomes limited as does
> the flexibility of future boards. I will continue to advocate for the slowing down of this process.
>
> Finally, while I absolutely support extracurricular and co-curricular programming and have personally
> studied their value, I also understand from the work of Yale-economist Barbara Biasi that facility
> investments in HVAC, STEM, building infrastructure, safety, and classrooms tend to have the greatest
> positive impact on student academic outcomes, whereas athletics-focused investments are more closely
> tied to increases in local property values. Through correspondence, she shared with me that none show
> an increase in enrollment. But as I wrote on the investment for the indoor pool in Coralville, my
> opinion can be changed when a need-based argument shows that a need will be met.
>
> With full respect to those who built and worked on these plans in the past and present and my
> colleagues here that do support the plan, I am sorry but I cannot support the FMP as it is currently
> proposed.
